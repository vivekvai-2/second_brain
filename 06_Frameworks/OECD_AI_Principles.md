# OECD AI Principles

**Type:** Framework **Status:** Active **Last Updated:** 2026-06-12

---

## Core Synthesis

The OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024) are the foundational international normative reference for ethical and trustworthy AI — the earliest intergovernmental instrument to articulate cross-sectoral AI governance principles and the most widely cited in comparative regulatory analysis. Within PRIS, they serve two distinct roles: as a P1 SLR anchor (the starting scaffold against which national and sectoral frameworks are mapped) and as an fsQCA case representing principles-based, non-binding Regulatory Logic at the international level. Their analytical significance lies less in their enforceability — they have none — and more in their norm-setting function: the OECD Principles have shaped the vocabulary of AI governance across jurisdictions including India's IndiaAI framework, the EU AI Act's foundational ethics layer, and NIST's RMF value alignment.

## Framework Identity

- **Issuing body:** Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)
- **Instrument type:** Intergovernmental Recommendation — non-binding soft law
- **Jurisdiction:** 38 OECD member countries (plus non-member adherents); adopted by G20 in 2019; referenced globally
- **Binding status:** Non-binding; operates through political commitment and norm diffusion
- **Key dates:**
    - May 2019: Original Principles adopted by OECD Council
    - 2021: OECD AI Policy Observatory launched as implementation tracking tool
    - 2024: Principles updated to incorporate generative AI and foundation model considerations _(verify exact update text via Perplexity)_

## Structural Overview

The Principles are organised into two tiers. The first tier comprises **five value-based principles** for responsible stewardship of trustworthy AI: inclusive growth and sustainable development; respect for the rule of law and human rights; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; and accountability. These are not operationalised as obligations — they establish a normative vocabulary rather than a compliance architecture.

The second tier comprises **five recommendations to governments** on AI policy and international co-operation: investing in AI research and development; fostering an enabling environment for AI deployment; shaping an enabling policy environment; building human capacity and preparing for labour market transformation; and co-operating internationally. This tier is explicitly addressed to states, not to AI developers or deployers, and reflects the OECD's regulatory-design orientation rather than enterprise-compliance orientation.

The structural logic is **principles proliferation**: the instrument generates normative vocabulary that downstream frameworks translate into obligations. This proliferation-then-operationalisation pattern is analytically significant for the P1 SLR typology (it maps to the Normative Anchor archetype) and for the fsQCA model (it marks the low-RL end of the Regulatory Logic spectrum — highly principled, minimally prescriptive).

## Regulatory Position & Comparative Significance

The OECD Principles occupy the international normative baseline position in the PRIS framework set. They predate all other cases and have been explicitly referenced or incorporated in NIST AI RMF, UNESCO Recommendation, EU AI Act recitals, IndiaAI guidelines, and — indirectly — in RBI FREE-AI's seven-sutra architecture. Their comparative significance within PRIS is therefore both historical (first-mover status shapes subsequent vocabulary) and structural (they define the principles-only, no-enforcement end of the RL spectrum against which the EU AI Act's binding risk-tiering is positioned). For the fsQCA model, they are likely to score high on EA (explicit ethical principles) and SI (intergovernmental consultation process) but low on SR (no stakeholder-role attribution) and RL (no enforcement mechanism).

## Key Tensions & Gaps

The core tension in the OECD Principles is the gap between normative aspiration and implementation operationalisation: the five value principles generate broad normative consensus but provide no mechanism for translating that consensus into enforceable obligations. The 2024 update addresses generative AI and foundation models at the principle level, but the same implementation gap persists — producing a document that tracks technological change in vocabulary while remaining structurally unable to discipline it. For jurisdictions that have cited the OECD Principles as the foundation for national AI strategies (India included), this gap is propagated downstream: the principles provide legitimacy without architecture. This tension is analytically productive for the P1 typology and the Regulatory Complexity Handbook.
