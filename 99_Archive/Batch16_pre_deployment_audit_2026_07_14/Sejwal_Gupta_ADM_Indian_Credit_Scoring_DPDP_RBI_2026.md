## Summary

Sejwal and Gupta assess the ADM regulatory landscape governing India's rapidly-expanding credit ecosystem — where algorithmic credit-scoring systems accelerate financial inclusion but also risk perpetuating systemic biases and undermining consumer trust. The paper's central tension: balancing high-velocity credit-decision infrastructure with the fundamental rights and protections owed to the data principal. It examines how the DPDP Act 2023 (horizontal) and RBI sector-specific guidance (Digital Lending Directions 2025, Credit Information Companies Regulations) interact across three interconnected dimensions — algorithmic fairness, system transparency, and privacy. The paper is the sharpest single academic paper in the corpus directly on the RBI × DPDP × ADM tension that this research programme foregrounds.

## Key Findings and Framework

### Three-Dimensional ADM Assessment Structure

| Dimension | DPDP Act 2023 governance | RBI Guidelines governance | Gap Identified |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Algorithmic Fairness** | Data-processing fairness principle; no explicit fairness obligation for ADM output | RBI Digital Lending Directions require creditworthiness assessment; no fairness-testing mandate | No mandatory fairness audit or protected-characteristic testing |
| **System Transparency** | Notice + consent under DPDP; no explainability right | RBI RU5 privacy policy disclosure for AI use; RBI FREE-AI aspirational explainability | No statutory ADM explanation right; disclosure exists but not actionable |
| **Privacy** | DPDP full statute; Rule 13(3) SDF algorithmic due diligence | Payment localisation; RBI outsourcing rules | Cross-instrument coordination gap; SDF DPIA vs RBI audit non-recognition |

### Central Argument

Indian credit-scoring ADM sits at the intersection of DPDP (horizontal privacy), RBI Digital Lending (sectoral), and Credit Information Companies Regulations (specialised) without a single instrument that governs ADM outputs comprehensively. The three-dimensional gap (fairness / transparency / privacy) generates constitutional and consumer-protection risks that will accumulate as automated credit-decisioning scales.

### Governance Prescription

Sejwal & Gupta propose four instrument-level reforms:
1. Statutory ADM explanation right (GDPR Art. 22 analogue in DPDP)
2. Mandatory fairness testing for high-impact credit ADM
3. Cross-recognition of RBI outsourcing audits + DPDP SDF audits
4. Sector-specific ADM ethics guidelines under RBI Master Direction rather than voluntary FREE-AI framework
