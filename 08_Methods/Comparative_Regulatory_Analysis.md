# Comparative Regulatory Analysis

**Type:** Method **Status:** Active — created 2026-08-02 (methods sourcing pass)
**Role:** the method PRIS has used throughout P3, P4 and the fsQCA chapter without ever having a methodological anchor

---

## Core Synthesis

Comparative regulatory analysis was the largest unanchored method in the vault. Every paper compares Indian instruments against EU, Singaporean, US and UK counterparts; no note recorded what licenses that comparison or what its limits are. This note records the functional-equivalence tradition that supplies the closest available scaffold — and states plainly that **the specific methodological problem this research faces has no published solution**, which converts a gap into a contribution opportunity.

## The Problem This Research Actually Has

PRIS routinely compares instruments of **radically different binding force**:

| Instrument | Binding status |
|---|---|
| GDPR | Binding Regulation, enforced, judicially elaborated |
| EU AI Act | Binding, but **zero cited harmonised standards** — conformity architecture inoperative |
| RBI Master Directions | Binding licensing conditions |
| RBI FREE-AI | Committee recommendations, no operative amendment |
| MAS FEAT / Veritas | Wholly advisory; bindingness via supervisory expectation only |
| IS/ISO/IEC 42001 | Voluntary standard, **recognised by no regulator anywhere** |
| DEPA | Never-finalised draft that became operative through other instruments |

Treating these as comparable requires a methodological warrant. **⚠ No dedicated methodological article theorising comparison of regulatory regimes where instruments differ in binding force was located.** The functional-equivalence literature addresses doctrinal difference between *statutes*; it does not treat binding-versus-advisory status as a methodological variable.

**Consequence: this bridge must be built, not cited.** The defensible construction is Michaels' "similarity in difference" — institutions are comparable if functionally equivalent, "similar in one function while potentially different in all other regards." Binding force becomes one of the "other regards," not a bar to comparison. That argument needs to be made explicitly in the methodology chapter rather than assumed, and it is itself a small methodological contribution.

## Method Profile

- **Type:** Comparative legal/regulatory method; functional comparison
- **Core principle:** functional equivalence — comparability rests on shared function, not shared doctrinal form
- **Scope in PRIS:** P4 (India vs EU/Singapore/UK benchmarking); P3 (BFSI cross-jurisdiction); fsQCA chapter (cross-case condition calibration)
- **Register discipline:** functional comparison is "strong as a tool for understanding, comparing, and critiquing different laws, but a weak tool for evaluating" them normatively (Michaels). P4's Section 5 redesign proposal is normative and therefore **exceeds what the functional method licenses** — it must be grounded in regulatory-governance theory rather than in comparison alone. Worth stating in the paper before a reviewer says it.

## The Six-Step Functional Procedure

Following Zweigert & Kötz as reconstructed by Mahy (2016):

1. Identify a shared social problem, **defined without recourse to legal terms**
2. Find and describe legal *and extra-legal* solutions across systems
3. Identify similarities and differences between solutions
4. Build a conceptual language for discussion
5. Explain similarities/differences by reference to wider context
6. Critically and normatively evaluate

Step 1 is the one PRIS most often skips. "How is AI governed in BFSI?" is a legally-framed question. The functionally correct framing is something closer to: *how does a society allocate responsibility when a consequential decision about a person is made by a system nobody fully understands?* Reframing at step 1 makes India's absence of an ADM right and the EU's Art. 22 prohibition comparable answers to one problem rather than a presence-versus-absence tally.

Also useful: the **external law / internal law** distinction — law as written versus law as it operates. Directly applicable to the recurring PRIS finding that instruments exist but do not operate (DPDP enacted-not-operative; the DPB constituted-but-unstaffed; FREE-AI recommended-not-amended).

## Literature Anchors

- **Michaels, R.** *The Functional Method of Comparative Law.* Oxford Handbook of Comparative Law; SSRN 2005. **The primary theoretical anchor** — critical reconstruction of functionalism; "similarity in difference"; the understanding-versus-evaluating limitation.
- **Mahy, P.** (2016). *The functional approach in comparative socio-legal research.* International Journal of Law in Context. — tests functionalism's limits for **empirical** rather than purely doctrinal work; source of the six-step formulation above. The closest published treatment to PRIS's actual use.
- Max Planck Encyclopedia of European Private Law, *Comparative Law* entry — citable reference-work statement.
- Emory University Libraries, *Foreign and Comparative Law Basics* (21 Sep 2020) — external/internal law distinction. ⚠ A research guide, not peer-reviewed; cite as practical supplement only.

## Open Methodological Question

**OQ-CRA-01 — the binding-force problem.** No source treats binding-versus-advisory status as a methodological variable in regulatory comparison. Given that the 2026-08-02 sourcing sequence established that two of P4's three benchmarks are far less binding than assumed (EU AI Act without harmonised standards; Singapore without any binding instrument), this is not a peripheral concern — it is central to whether P4's comparisons hold. Constructing the warrant explicitly, from Michaels, is required work and is publishable in its own right.
