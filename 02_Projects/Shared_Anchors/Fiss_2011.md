# Fiss (2011)

**Full citation:** Fiss, P. C. (2011). Building better causal theories: A fuzzy set approach to typologies in organization research. *Academy of Management Journal*, *54*(2), 393–420.  
**Zotero key:** `fiss_2011`  
**Journal:** Academy of Management Journal (ABDC-A*)

---

## Core Argument

Typologies in organisation research describe *what configurations of attributes co-occur* across cases (descriptive contribution). fsQCA identifies *what configurations of conditions are sufficient for outcomes* (causal contribution). These are complementary, not competing, contributions. The optimal research programme uses SLR to build the typology and fsQCA to test which configurations are causally sufficient — the typology provides the analytical vocabulary; the causal analysis provides the explanatory mechanism.

---

## Why This Work Matters to PRIS

Fiss (2011) is the **theoretical bridge** connecting P1 (the SLR-derived AI governance typology) and the fsQCA empirical thesis chapter (causal configurations for legitimate outcomes). It provides the logical architecture that makes the two-paper design coherent: P1 is not just a literature review — it is the typology-building stage of a configurational research programme. The fsQCA chapter is not just an empirical analysis — it is the causal-testing stage.

This logic also rescued the GIQ paper from narrative architecture failure: the GIQ desk rejection was partly attributable to framing the SLR as a standalone descriptive contribution rather than as the first stage of a Fiss-logic configurational programme. The R&G resubmission of P1 must foreground the Fiss logic explicitly.

### Definitions and Taxonomy (not previously captured in this note)

- **Typologies:** conceptually derived interrelated sets of ideal types, each representing a unique combination of organizational attributes believed to determine the outcome(s) of interest.
- **Monothetic typologies:** each feature is necessary for membership and the set of features is jointly sufficient.
- **Polythetic typologies:** can be formed from different combinations of values on the features of interest — this is the type fsQCA is built to analyse.
- **Core elements:** causal conditions under which the evidence indicates a strong causal relationship with the outcome.
- **Peripheral elements:** causal conditions for which the evidence of a causal relationship is weaker.
- **Equifinality:** a system can reach the same final state from different initial conditions via different paths.
- **First-order equifinality:** equifinal types with different core characteristics (e.g., type A vs. type B).
- **Second-order equifinality:** neutral permutations *within* a given first-order equifinal type (e.g., type A1 vs. A2…An) — peripheral conditions that vary without affecting the configuration's overall performance.
- **Causal asymmetry:** the causes of an outcome's presence may be entirely different from the causes of its absence — directly relevant to PRIS's own "presence vs. absence of legitimate AI governance outcome" design.

### The Three Propositions (verbatim)

1. "Typological configurations will be characterized by a core and a periphery."
2. "Core and periphery in typological configurations will frequently exhibit neutral permutations."
3. "Typological configurations will be marked by causal asymmetry."

### Empirical Application (Miles & Snow Typology, UK High-Tech Manufacturing)

Fiss operationalises the core-periphery/equifinality/causal-asymmetry propositions on the Miles & Snow (1978) typology (Prospector/Analyzer/Defender/Reactor) using a sample of 205 UK high-technology manufacturing firms surveyed in 1999 (14% response rate; valid fsQCA sample of 139 cases after imputation for missing performance data). Truth table built from 2^k rows; lowest acceptable solution consistency threshold >0.80; minimum solution frequency of 3. High-performance configurations covered ~36% of outcome membership (consistency/coverage figures reported separately for high, very-high, and low/not-high performance solutions). Fuzzy calibration used percentile-anchored ROA thresholds (e.g., high-performance crossover at 12% ROA; very-high at 16.3%; low at 3.9%), each condition (formalization, centralization, administrative complexity, size, strategy factors, environmental rate of change) calibrated via named survey-scale/percentile methods with Cronbach's alpha reported per scale (0.74–0.86 range).

### Data-Quality Note (VERIFY-equivalent, flagged not silently corrected)

**Sample-size internal inconsistency:** The main text states a valid fsQCA sample of "139 cases" used for all subsequent analyses (after missing-data imputation), but Appendix A's cluster-analysis results report group sizes of N=93 (Prospector), N=41 (Analyzer), and N=59 (Defender) — summing to 193, not 139. This is an arithmetic contradiction in the source document itself, not a PRIS transcription error. Do not cite either figure as the paper's definitive sample size without independent resolution against the published (not pre-print) version.

**Publication-date note:** The source PDF's header cites "*Academy of Management Journal* · April 2011" but an internal title page reads "in press," suggesting the extracted document is a pre-print/working version rather than the final typeset article. The Method/Where-Used citation above (54(2), 393–420) reflects the confirmed published version; use that citation form, not the pre-print header.
