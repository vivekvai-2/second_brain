| **Regulatory Governance Theory** (supporting) | Provides the mechanism for jurisdiction allocation and coordination design; polycentric regulatory design; regulatory space fragmentation | P12 (Scott 2001/2002); Quaglia 2022; James & Quaglia 2024; Metrick & Tarullo 2021 (Cluster 3); Llewellyn 2005 (Cluster 3) |

**Theoretical gap flag (from `theoretical_scaffolding_v2.md` Part 5):**

> The **inter-regulator coordination mechanism** is theoretically under-specified. Institutional Theory explains *why* fragmentation persists (divergent logics). Regulatory Governance Theory explains *what kind* of coordination is needed. But neither specifies the *institutional machinery* — the governance body, decision rights, escalation procedures that would sustain coordination between two autonomous regulators with independent legal authority and different statutory mandates.
>
> **The paper must take an explicit position on this before Section 5 is drafted (PD-01).** Options:
> - **Concrete mechanism:** Propose an inter-regulator AI coordination committee under FSDC, with specified membership, decision-making rules (consensus vs lead-regulator), and coordination triggers. This is the higher-ambition position.
> - **Normative design proposal:** Frame the coordination design as prescriptive — what policymakers *should* consider — without claiming feasibility has been verified. This is the lower-risk position and is defensible for a doctrinal paper without regulatory interviews.
>
> Both are legally defensible in IJLIT. The concrete mechanism is a stronger contribution but risks appearing aspirational if India's regulatory political economy makes it infeasible. The normative framing is more honest about the paper's scope (doctrinal analysis establishes structural conditions; empirical validation is future work).
