## Substantive Content

### Policy Statement

The EO declares that United States policy is to **maintain global leadership in AI** through a **minimally burdensome national framework** that avoids a fragmented patchwork of 50+ State AI laws. The EO frames State-level AI regulation as a threat to US AI competitiveness and economic leadership.

### Operative Provisions (Section-by-Section)

| Section | Directive | Target Actor | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sec. 3 | Establish **AI Litigation Task Force** — challenge State AI laws considered to create onerous burdens | Attorney General | Within 30 days of EO |
| Sec. 4 | Evaluate State AI laws for "onerous" nature; coordinate Federal response | Secretary of Commerce | 90 days |
| Sec. 5 | Condition **BEAD program** broadband infrastructure funding on States not having "onerous AI laws" | Commerce (BEAD program administrator) | Ongoing |
| Sec. 6 | Initiate FCC proceeding to establish Federal AI **reporting and disclosure standard** (preempting State equivalents) | FCC Chairman | No specified deadline |
| Sec. 7 | Issue policy statement on preemption of State laws that effectively mandate "deceptive conduct" in AI models | FTC Chair | No specified deadline |
| Sec. 8 | Recommend to Congress: enact uniform Federal AI governance framework expressly preempting conflicting State AI laws | President via legislative recommendation | Legislative timeline |

### Carve-Outs (Explicit Exclusions from Preemption Scope)

Three categories are explicitly excluded from the "onerous AI laws" framing and therefore not subject to preemption challenge:

1. **Child safety** — State AI laws protecting minors from harm not targeted
2. **AI compute and data centre infrastructure** — State laws governing physical AI infrastructure (power, land use) excluded
3. **State government AI procurement** — States' own procurement and internal deployment of AI systems excluded

### Definition of "Onerous AI Laws"

The EO does not provide a precise statutory definition of "onerous." The AG and Commerce Secretary are directed to develop criteria. The absence of a definition is a deliberate drafting choice — it preserves executive discretion in choosing which State laws to challenge.

### Significance of AI Litigation Task Force (Sec. 3)

The establishment of an AI Litigation Task Force within the AG's office — with a 30-day formation deadline — is the most immediately actionable provision. It signals that the Federal Government intends to use litigation (likely pre-emption and Supremacy Clause challenges) to invalidate State AI laws rather than waiting for Congressional preemption legislation. As of the EO's December 2025 date, over 30 US States had enacted or were advancing AI-specific legislation.

### FCC and FTC Provisions (Secs. 6 and 7)

Sec. 6 (FCC) targets State laws requiring AI **disclosure** in communications (e.g., AI-generated calls, deepfake disclosure requirements in political advertising). Federal FCC standard would preempt State disclosure mandates.

Sec. 7 (FTC) targets State laws that, in the FTC's view, effectively require AI systems to engage in "deceptive conduct" — framed as State laws mandating certain AI behaviours that conflict with Federal deception standards.

## Analytical Significance for PRIS Research

### For P4 — Doctrinal / Comparative AI Governance (IJLIT)

This EO establishes the US federal approach to AI regulatory design as **explicitly preemptive and centralist** — the opposite of India's fragmented/parallel multi-regulator approach. The comparative contrast is analytically rich:

| Dimension | India | United States (Trump Dec 2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Regulatory architecture | Multi-regulator parallelism (SEBI, RBI, MeitY, CERT-In) | Federal centralisation; active preemption of sub-national variation |
| Coordination mechanism | Proposed NCAIC (not yet operative) | AI Litigation Task Force + FCC/FTC federal standard-setting |
| Burden orientation | Compliance cost acknowledged but no relief mechanism | Explicitly minimally burdensome; "onerous" laws challenged |
| Legal force | Sector-specific regulations with statutory backing | EO directing federal agencies; preemption via litigation and funding conditionality |

### For P1 — SLR (Regulation & Governance)

Provides the US data point for the global AI governance regulatory design survey. The December 2025 EO represents the most developed articulation of the Trump Administration's AI governance philosophy: pro-innovation, federal-centralising, preemptive. Paired with EU AI Act (risk-tier harmonisation), UK (pro-innovation, sector-led), Singapore (risk-proportionate guidance), and India (sectoral parallelism) for five-jurisdiction comparative analysis.

### For fsQCA — Regulatory Configurations

**US Configuration (post-Dec 2025 EO):** Federal-preemptive, minimally burdensome, no mandatory AI safety requirements, deregulatory at Federal level while actively suppressing State regulation. This is a distinct configurational pathway from EU (harmonised risk-tier), Singapore (risk-proportionate voluntary-to-mandatory arc), and India (fragmented sector-specific). Strengthens the comparative validity of fsQCA's multi-jurisdiction truth table.

### Coordination / Parallelism Pattern

**Not directly applicable** — the EO is specifically an **anti-parallelism mechanism**: it seeks to eliminate the 50-State AI regulatory fragmentation in the US. It therefore serves as a **negative comparator** for India's multi-regulator parallelism — the US is attempting to solve the fragmentation problem that India has not yet addressed.
