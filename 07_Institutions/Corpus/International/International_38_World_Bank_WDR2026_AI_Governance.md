### Core framing: three government roles, three-stage maturity ladder
The report separates government posture into three functional roles — **Enabler** (infrastructure/skills investment), **User** (AI procurement and deployment in public service delivery), **Regulator** (market-corrective rulemaking) — and a maturity sequencing logic, **Adopt → Adapt → Advance**. The "User" role is treated as functionally distinct from "Regulator," a separation absent from OECD/NIST/EU AI Act framing in this vault's existing International nodes — government-as-procurer is argued to have larger near-term welfare effects in low-capacity states than formal regulation.

### AI governance life cycle (Ch.9, Figure 9.2)
Staged, iterative process rather than a single legislative act, wrapped throughout by ongoing **international cooperation** [WDR p.313–314]:

1. **Assess landscape** — map de jure and de facto reality of laws, institutions, enforcement [WDR p.313]. *Evidence: UK's 2023 AI white paper stocktake, requiring regulators to evaluate frameworks against five cross-sectoral principles [WDR p.315].*
2. **Identify gaps** — pinpoint shortfalls "both on paper and in practice," across AI's input/process/output layers [WDR p.313, 317].
3. **Extend/clarify or make instruments** — extend existing instruments first; new instruments only for genuinely unaddressed harms [WDR p.313]. *Evidence: California AI Transparency Act (training-data disclosure) as an "extend" example [WDR p.318].*
4. **Build enforcement capacity** — regulator funding, technical capacity, sandboxes [WDR p.313]. *Evidence: GRIDMAP shows enforcement gaps persist even where legal frameworks are strong (Figure 9.3) [WDR p.317].*
5. **Build compliance capacity** — technical standards to build firm/public-sector practice (e.g., human rights impact assessments) [WDR p.313–314].
6. **Monitor and adapt** — track outcomes, feed back into the cycle [WDR p.313].

**Capacity-contingent emphasis:** high-capacity states prioritize stages 3–5; low-capacity states focus on stages 1–2 and lean on voluntary international standards for stage 5 [WDR p.313] — this is the report's operational definition of its "voluntary standards first" default.

### International cooperation architecture + Box 9.4
`[WDR-sourced only — no fresher post-publication confirmation was found in this session's search pass for these four instruments; do not treat as independently re-verified]`
- **UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance** — forum for governments, civil society, private sector, science community "on equal footing," designed to include developing-country voices.
- **Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI** — described by WDR as the first binding international treaty on AI; signatories commit to AI activity compatible with human rights, democracy, rule of law, while retaining national implementation flexibility.
- **Cartagena de Indias Declaration** — adopted August 2024, 17 Latin American/Caribbean countries; coordinated ethical/responsible/inclusive AI governance and education.
- **Africa Declaration on Artificial Intelligence** — adopted April 2025, Global AI Summit on Africa, Kigali; 49 African countries + African Union; human dignity, rights, freedoms, environmental sustainability.
- **Box 9.4 — California/China convergence:** WDR frames California and China as parallel — not opposed — leaders in *both* AI innovation and *binding* AI regulation, complicating a standard "innovation vs. regulation" dichotomy. California passed 13 AI bills in 2025 via a transparent, participatory process (building on its 2018 data-protection law); China was an early mover on binding AI law with separate statutes for recommendation algorithms, deep synthesis, and generative AI. WDR's normative conclusion: developing countries should extract common principles rather than copy either single model.

### Two-layer federated coordination model (Spotlight 7, Table S7.1)
**Horizontal layer** (whole-of-government direction): Center-of-Government module (PM/president's office anchor — strong signaling, bottleneck risk); Lead-Agency module (designated ministry/statutory body — technical depth, contested peer-ministry authority); Interministerial Council module (standing alignment body — preserves sectoral autonomy, slow consensus).
**Vertical layer** (line-ministry deployment): Extension module (absorb AI mandate into existing institutions — efficient, overstretch risk); Specialization module (new dedicated institution — signals seriousness, high cost, "AI-washing" risk if under-resourced).

### GRIDMAP and Chapter 8 capacity findings
**GRIDMAP** (Global Regulations, Institutional Development, and Market Authorities Perspective) [WDR Fig. 9.3, p.317]. ✅ **Flag closed 2026-08-09 — with one material scope narrowing.**

🔴 **Scope correction.** The 2026-08-05 node described GRIDMAP as assessing "safe, trustworthy **AI** markets." It does not. Figure 9.3's caption is: *"Countries across income groups struggle to implement and enforce **data protection** regulations in practice, even where legal frameworks are robust on paper."* The instrument is the **GRIDMAP Data Markets Module** (World Bank 2025b), not an AI-market assessment. The de jure/de facto finding is real and citable — but it is a **data-protection** finding used in Ch.9 as an *analogy* for what AI enforcement will face, not direct evidence about AI regimes. Citing it as AI evidence would be an overreach.

Confirmed detail:
- **Three assessed dimensions:** legal framework · institutional arrangements · **implementation and enforcement**
- **First wave 2025, 53 economies**, concentrated in Europe and Central Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, and Sub-Saharan Africa
- Assessment is **survey-based**, completed by the relevant regulatory authorities themselves — a self-report design worth flagging in any methods discussion
- **Headline:** economies across **all** income groups fall below the minimum package, and the shortfall widens as you move from legal framework → institutional arrangements → implementation and enforcement. **The de jure/de facto gap is universal, not developing-country-specific.** This is the single most transferable finding in Ch.9 for [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Governance_Debt]].
- **Illustrative case (p.317):** a **€20 million** fine issued in 2023 by the Italian data protection regulator against Clearview AI — for unlawfully processing biometric and geolocation data of people in Italy — **remains unenforced, and the unlawfully processed data have yet to be deleted.** The report's framing is pointed: "the large asymmetries in resources and knowledge between industry and regulators means this is also a problem for developed countries."

**Figure 9.4 — regulator budget adequacy, newly captured 2026-08-09.** Responses to *"The Authority has sufficient budget to carry out the activities associated with handling complaints, investigations, hiring staff, and delivering training programs":* **43% of data protection authorities in low-income countries *strongly disagree*, against 15% in high-income countries** [WDR p.322, from World Bank 2025b]. Most consumer- and data-protection authorities worldwide report insufficient resources; the income gradient is the finding. The report's remedy is specific and institutional rather than merely fiscal: **multiyear rather than annual budget cycles**, and approval procedures that insulate regulator funding "from changes in political will and from private influence."

**Chapter 8 (Governments as Users), partial retrieval only:** procurement framed as a governance lever — governments can withhold public spending as a compliance mechanism. US 2025 OMB guidance for "high-impact AI" requires continuous impact assessments, human oversight, appeal mechanisms, cited as a procurement-side instrument. Lower-income economies specifically lag in **assurance practices** (testing, evaluation, impact-assessment routines) even where procurement policy exists on paper — the same de jure/de facto gap GRIDMAP documents. Figure 9.1 (58-economy survey): ~43% of low-/lower-middle-income respondents cite "absence of relevant policies, frameworks, or standards" as their top AI-adoption constraint, versus data-privacy/ethical/legal concerns dominating in higher-income economies [WDR p.309]. **Limitation:** full Ch.8 text (procurement case studies, detailed capacity metrics) was not fully retrievable in the extraction pass behind this update — recommend a targeted follow-up fetch of the standalone Ch.8 PDF.

- **NIST AI RMF remains v1.0** (Jan 2023 core, no 2.0), maturing via profiles (Critical Infrastructure Profile concept note, Apr 2026; AI Agent Standards Initiative announced Feb 2026, Interoperability Profile due Q4 2026) — relevant comparator for the Ch.9 life-cycle model above.

## Substantive Content — Primary-Text Pass, 2026-08-09

*Everything in this section is grounded in the report text supplied 2026-08-09. It covers the roughly 85% of the report the 2026-08-05 node did not reach. Page numbers are from the published pagination.*

### The report's own frame, in its own words

The **Foreword** (Indermit Gill, Senior Vice President and Chief Economist) is unusually blunt about audience and posture: *"The debates over artificial intelligence have devolved into exercises in self-absorption in most developed economies… Developing economies should tune out much of this noise."* The organising counsel is that developing economies should be *"blinded neither by the hype nor the hysteria."*

Three recommendations in the Foreword are policy-directional and quotable:
- **"predictive AI in back-end functions instead of generative AI at the frontline of service delivery"** — a direct challenge to how most AI-in-government pilots are being scoped
- **"making interoperability a feature instead of aiming for sovereignty"** — the report's core answer to the dependency problem, with **UPI and Brazil's Pix named as the model**
- **"small AI applications diffused widely across the economy instead of big investments in large language models concentrated in a few firms"**

The historical warning is stated as an arithmetic: *"At its outset, Britain was roughly four times richer per person than India. By the end of the 20th century, it was 10 times richer."*

### Ch.9 — three additions the 2026-08-05 pass missed entirely

**1. "Regulatory pluralism … underpinned by a shared AI governance floor" (p. 326).** This is the report's normative resolution of the fragmentation problem, and it is a **named theoretical position**, not a throwaway:

> *"Importantly, global dialogue should aim for regulatory pluralism — allowing countries to shape national governance frameworks in ways that match local communities' needs — as its goal, but underpinned by a shared AI governance floor, where countries agree to ensure a common level of human rights protection and interoperability of different governance regimes."*

Sourced to **Schüür, Birtwistle, Field Reid, Groves and Rincon (2026), Ada Lovelace Institute**, proposed as an organising concept for the UN Global Dialogue. **This inverts how [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Regulatory_Parallelism]] is currently framed in the vault** — parallelism as pathology becomes parallelism as *normatively acceptable, conditional on a floor*. See the concept note for the recorded implication.

**2. Box 9.4 directly contests the three-model regulatory-competition thesis (pp. 326–327).** The box is titled *"Beyond narratives of regulatory competition: California and China as leaders in both innovation and regulation."* Its argument:

> *"this narrative of regulatory competition misses the fact that leading jurisdictions for the development of AI, such as California and China, have more common ground than initially meets the eye."*

Evidence marshalled: California passed **13 AI-related bills in 2025** covering safety and transparency obligations, child safety, data rights and consumer protection, having been the first US state to pass comprehensive data-protection law in 2018; China introduced **separate binding instruments for recommendation algorithms, deep-synthesis technologies, and generative AI services**. Both lead on private AI investment, patent filings and notable frontier models. The normative conclusion:

> *"for developing countries, the choice is less about which regulatory model (market based, rights based, or state based) and more about finding common ground with other countries around the need for good governance to promote responsible innovation."*

**Bradford appears nowhere in this vault.** Box 9.4 is a World Bank flagship pushing back on the dominant comparative framework, which makes it a positioning asset for P1 rather than mere background.

### 🔷 The assurance-reuse **inversion** — strongest single finding of this pass

Ch.9, *"Build compliance capacity with AI providers and deployers using voluntary standards"* (p. 324), records two mechanisms by which jurisdictions grant **legal credit for third-party standards compliance**:

| Jurisdiction | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Colorado** (SB24-205, Consumer Protections for Artificial Intelligence) and **Texas** (Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act) | Compliance with the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** operates as *"a **shield from legal liability**"* | Third-party framework adherence substitutes for independent regulatory scrutiny |
| **EU AI Act** | Standards developed by CEN and CENELEC are *"one route for AI providers to take to demonstrate compliance with regulatory requirements"* | Presumption of conformity — though see [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_33_CEN_CENELEC_JTC21_Harmonised_Standards_2026]]: **zero AI-specific harmonised standards have actually been cited in the Official Journal**, so the presumption currently attaches to nothing |

**Set against India.** [[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_14_Draft_Guidance_Model_Risk_Management_2026]] para 46(i) requires independent validation *"**notwithstanding** any validation, certification, or assurance provided by the third-party provider."*

**This converts [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Standard/Assurance_Reuse_Gap]] from a deficit finding into a divergence finding.** The construct has been carried in the vault as an *absence* — regulators failing to recognise assurance, inferred from silence. It is now demonstrable that **two US states and the EU affirmatively grant safe harbour or conformity presumption on the basis of voluntary-standards compliance, while India's most recent draft expressly refuses it.** That is a positional difference between jurisdictions, not a maturity gap. Recorded at the concept note.

The report also supplies the **counterweight argument** for why voluntary standards cannot stand alone (p. 311): *"Most AI providers are private sector actors largely driven by profit incentives that do not consider the social and political costs of AI"*; Acemoglu (2023) on innovation direction becoming *"systematically distorted"*; and the ethics-washing risk (Veale et al. 2023). Anthropic cofounder **Christopher Olah** is quoted acknowledging that AI companies face *"a set of incentives and constraints that can sometimes conflict with doing the right thing"* — delivered at the launch of **Pope Leo XIV's May 2026 encyclical** on AI harms (Rich, Povoledo and Dias 2026). That encyclical is a corpus candidate in its own right.

### Ch.9 — evidence-contestation instance worth keeping for methods

**Note 31** documents a live dispute over EU AI Act compliance costs: the **Centre for Data Innovation** claimed compliance could cost the EU economy **€31 billion**; the **Centre for European Policy Studies** — which authored the original compliance-cost study for the Commission's own Impact Assessment — responded that CDI had *"extrapolated costs based on factually incorrect assumptions and had recompiled statistics from the original study in a misleading way."* A documented instance of contested cost evidence in AI regulatory analysis, usable in the P1 methods-critique section.

The report's own framing of the underlying difficulty is quotable: policy makers face a **pacing problem** — *"Laws, rules, and regulations take time to design, draft, and implement, whereas AI is being developed and deployed at breakneck speed"* — and the UN's **Preliminary Report of the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI** (July 2026, 40 scientists across five UN regions) is cited as recognising the same evidence dilemma: *"policy makers need evidence to make consequential governance decisions; however, by the time the evidence emerges, it may be too late to act."* The report's answer is the **precautionary principle**, quoting WDR 2025: *"it is sensible to implement standards before harm becomes irreversible."*

### Ch.9 — harms taxonomy across AI's operational layers

The report structures governance gaps by **input / process / output** layer, which is a usable coding frame:

- **Input layer** — training-data transparency (**California AI Transparency Act** requires generative AI developers to publish documentation on sources, ownership and IP status of training data); community-level data governance; labour regulation; safe reuse of sensitive public-sector data (**EU Data Governance Act**)
- **Process layer** — the black-box problem for auditing and compliance verification
- **Output layer** — procurement guardrails for high-impact AI; cybersecurity obligations; red lines on unacceptable uses; sectoral regulation

**Box 9.3 — community data licensing, directly relevant to P2.** The **Nwulite Obodo Open Data License (NOODL)** applies *differentiated* terms by user origin: African and developing-country users may reuse and distribute within those regions under share-alike terms, with commercial use restricted to markets outside those regions; users elsewhere must additionally provide **royalty payments or other benefits** to the African data-set providers. New Zealand's **Kaitiakitanga License** for Māori te reo speech data (2,500+ community contributors) prohibits surveillance, tracking and discrimination, and restricts third-party model-building on the corpus. **Creative Commons' CC Signals** and the **Mozilla Data Collective** are cited as parallel mechanisms. The report is explicit that this is contested terrain: open licensing without modification *"may expose local communities and marginalized groups to extractive practices from actors with greater resources."*

**Peru is a governance instance the vault does not hold** (p. 321): Law 31814 (2023) and its implementing decree (Supreme Decree 115-2025-PCM, September 2025) mandate **regular digital-security audits** of public-administration AI to identify vulnerabilities and biases, and require use of technical standards for information-security controls. Structurally close to what RBI_14 does for models — and a candidate corpus node.

### Ch.8 — Governments as Users (the node previously held only partial extraction)

**Table 8.1 — readiness-to-strategy matrix.** The report is directive about matching ambition to capacity:

| Stage of AI readiness | Adopt | Adapt | Advance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Early digital government** — partly paper-based, weak oversight | ★ **Priority starting point** | Targeted pilots | ✗ **Not recommended** |
| **Connected digital government** — established administrative systems, functioning e-gov platforms | ✓ Feasible | ★ **Priority starting point** | Rare; strong case required |
| **Advanced digital government** — interoperable, well-governed data, mature platforms | ✓ Feasible | ✓ **Default for many high-value uses** | ★ Selective strategic investment only |

The warning attached is political-economy: *"National language models, dedicated compute infrastructure, and locally trained predictive systems generate political visibility more readily than the slower work of building capable teams and improving data quality. But political visibility is not the same as public value."* **Singapore's SEA-LION is offered as the counter-example of institutional humility** — it began aiming to train models from scratch and **shifted to customising existing open models** as better ones became available.

**🔷 Box 8.3 — project model versus product model.** This is the most transferable governance argument in Ch.8 and it has no equivalent anywhere in the vault:

> *"Governments fund technology as if it were static, following a project model that is both expensive and more prone to failure… AI intensifies this mismatch. The dominant costs are ongoing, not up-front; however, the project model funds the launch as if it were the largest cost."*

Evidence: Flyvbjerg and Budzier — **one in six IT projects ends in cost overruns of about 200%**, following a power-law distribution. Practitioner estimate cited (Pahlka 2023): most government technology could deliver **85% of required functionality at 10% of typical project cost** if governments started smaller and iterated. The prescription is a **standing budget line, a permanent internal team, and a mandate that does not expire when the contract is signed.** The failure mode named is precise: *"AI systems that are not actively maintained degrade over time. Model versions deteriorate, performance drifts, and security vulnerabilities accumulate."* **This is [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Governance_Debt]] stated in budgetary terms** — governments paying twice, once to build and again to repair a system that has drifted beyond control.

**Procurement principles for preserving agency (p. 295).** Three, stated as design rules:
1. **Data and context must be portable** — interaction logs, administrative decisions, operational manuals and configuration choices belong to the government and must be exportable. Otherwise *"a government that switches providers starts from zero."*
2. **Contracts should assume a future in which different AI models are used** — no exclusivity clauses; interoperable interfaces; migration paths with safeguards against price escalation and a right to retender.
3. **Lock-in should never be the default** — for identity verification, eligibility decisions and security systems, the cost of both lock-in and failure is far higher, so these must remain switchable.

The report also flags **pre-contractual lock-in**, which most procurement rules do not reach: *"Low-cost pilots, vendor partnerships, and donor-funded packages let a system accumulate the interaction logs, configurations, and integrations that make it useful… by the time a formal contract is signed the vendor has been chosen in all but name."* **Directly relevant to donor-funded AI deployments in the DPI space.**

**Market-shaping levers.** Public procurement averages **12.7% of GDP across OECD members and 15–22% in many developing countries**. Two mechanisms are proposed: **advance market commitments** (the GAVI analogy) and **pooled cross-border ownership** — Estonia, Finland and Iceland jointly own and operate **X-Road** through the **Nordic Institute for Interoperability Solutions**. France's open-source procurement preference (2012) is cited as evidence that procurement diffuses standards beyond government: it increased open-source contributions by **more than 1.1 million per year** and grew domestic digital-sector employment (Nagle 2023).

**🔷 Box 8.1 — shadow AI. Hard numbers, and they are bad.**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Digital agencies in **lower-middle-income** economies with enterprise AI licences | **33%** |
| **Low-income** economies with any enterprise AI licence | **None** |
| Low- and lower-middle-income economies with **formal AI guidelines** | **17%** (48% still drafting) |
| Judicial workers across 96 countries who had **used** AI tools (UNESCO, n=560) | **44%** |
| …who had received **any guidance** on appropriate use | **9%** |
| Employees globally preferring **free public** AI tools over employer-provided (n=32,000, 47 countries) | **~70%** |
| …who had **uploaded sensitive organisational data** to public platforms | **~50%** |

The report's public-sector framing is the sharp part: *"In the public sector, this practice can expose highly sensitive citizen data such as tax records, law enforcement histories, or case files to unvetted consumer tools with no audit trail."* It also notes that **licences alone do not eliminate shadow use** — habituation to repeated warnings is documented — and recommends treating guidelines as **behavioural interventions to be evaluated**, not safeguards whose existence confers protection. **BBVA's use-it-or-lose-it licence allocation** (scaling 3,000 → 11,000 users in about a year via peer diffusion through power users) is the cited counter-design. **Directly actionable against [[07_Institutions/Corpus/Judiciary/SCI_1_White_Paper_AI_and_Judiciary_2025]].**

**🔷 Box 8.2 — RADAR, a benchmark instrument the vault should track.** Jordan, Ramos-Maqueda and Peixoto (2026) tested four frontier LLMs across **166 countries** on citizen-style questions about 20 public services, in two modes: **chat** (does the model give correct country-specific guidance?) and **agent** (can automated agents navigate government portals?). Two findings:
1. Chat scores are **highly predicted by the UN E-Government Development Index**; agent scores are **far less predictable**, meaning *"specific choices about the ways in which government portals are engineered drive most of the variation."*
2. **English-speaking countries score 0.2–0.4 points higher after controlling for GDP per capita** and other readiness metrics — *"a gap [that] might reflect the composition of AI training data more than the quality of government services."*

**India appears among the overperformers** in the chat-score scatter. The remedies are low-cost: crawlable text-accessible official pages, stable links, step-by-step procedures, consistent information architecture, and security configurations that do not inadvertently block legitimate AI agents. Preprint: manuelramosmaqueda.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/radar_arxiv_preprint.pdf

**Machine-to-machine government access — a governance frontier the vault has not recorded.** *"Public infrastructure will be used by private AI providers whether governments plan for it or not."* The requirements named are **verifiable credentials** authenticating agents, explicit definition of what actions agents may take on a citizen's behalf, and **auditable action records**. The closing line is a positioning claim: *"Governments that design for secure, authorized machine-to-machine interaction will set the terms of that access; those that do not will leave private providers to shape how citizens interact with the state."* Sourced to Ilves, Kilian, Parazzoli, Peixoto and Velsberg (2025), *The Agentic State*. **Cross-reference [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Decision_Infrastructure]] — this is the H2 workflow→decision handoff appearing in a multilateral policy text.**

**Data-readiness detail relevant to [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Records_Rules_Asymmetry]].** Generative AI now makes digitisation of large-scale historical tables **50 times less expensive** than traditional outsourcing while reducing the share of tables with structural errors **from 61.4% to 0.35%** (Bäcker-Peral, Meursault and Severen 2026). Brazil's **VICTOR** system reduced Supreme Federal Court appeal-eligibility determination **from 40 minutes to 5 seconds**. But the report also warns that administrative data *"reflect the workflows that generate them, not necessarily the underlying phenomena policy makers seek to measure"* — uneven uptake across offices produces **non-random missingness**, so *"AI risks learning who gets measured rather than the phenomenon of interest."* The arrest-data-as-crime-proxy example (Ensign et al. 2017; Neil and Zanger-Tishler 2025) is the illustration.

### Ch.5 — Social Impact: the front-end / back-end split

**Procurement-contract evidence base (n = 6,127,564 contracts classified using GPT-5):**

| Dimension | Finding |
|---|---|
| AI systems built for **internal (back-end)** government functions | **88.7%** |
| Systems using **predictive or optimisation** models | **78.5%** |
| Systems using **generative** AI | **7.6%** — almost entirely front-end |

Back-end procurement in low- and lower-middle-income countries concentrates on predictive analytics and decision support (**35.5%**), remote sensing and geospatial intelligence (**15.0%**), and environmental monitoring and early warning.

**Kenya's judicial mediation-assignment algorithm** is the report's flagship back-end case and is directly relevant to [[07_Institutions/Corpus/Judiciary/SCI_1_White_Paper_AI_and_Judiciary_2025]]: the **Cadaster** platform assigns **10,000+ cases per year to 1,500+ mediators**, cutting assignment time from up to 10 days to under one day, with an expected **increase in case resolution rates of up to 8%** (Farabi et al. 2026). Design detail that matters for governance: the system is *"explicitly built to learn about new mediators without making any assumptions based on personal characteristics, and human court officers make all final decisions regarding assignments."*

**🔷 Box 5.3 — the evidence base is thinner than almost anyone assumes.** Screening 280,000+ papers (March 2026, GPT-5-assisted), of which ~9,762 concerned AI health interventions:

| Filter | Remaining |
|---|---|
| AI-based digital health interventions | **9,762 studies** |
| …supporting frontline providers at point of contact | **22%** |
| …empirically tested | **<4%** |
| …conducted in low-resource contexts | **<1%** → **57 studies** |
| …providing **causal** evidence on health outcomes, care quality or efficiency | **17 studies** |

**Seventeen studies worldwide.** This is the single most useful number in the report for justifying an evidence-gap claim in P1, and for arguing that AI-in-public-services policy is running far ahead of its evidence base.

**Box 5.4 — Bangladesh phone-based poverty targeting, a documented AI failure.** Phone-based targeting achieved **32% recall** against proxy-means testing's **52%** — meaning 68% of poor households were missed and 68% of programme funds went to ineligible households. Random distribution would have achieved 21%. Only **45% of households perceived it as fair.** 6% of households could not be linked to phone records at all. **A rare, well-documented case of AI underperforming the conventional method it was meant to replace**, and a useful corrective to the Togo Novissi success story the same chapter reports.

**Box 5.1 — tuberculosis CAD cost-effectiveness**, comparing India (Maharashtra HTA: CAD both more accurate *and* cheaper than radiologist reading) against Nigeria (portable AI X-ray in lung health camps: **eight additional TB cases detected** at significantly raised screening cost). Same technology, opposite cost-effectiveness verdicts, driven entirely by what it is being compared against. **A clean illustration that AI evaluation is counterfactual-dependent** — the report's Spotlight 6 makes the same point as a general rule: *"an AI solution should be compared with the best available human or technical alternative; the goal is not a perfect system but to enable AI that produces net positive outcomes."* That four-stage evaluation chain — define objectives and benchmark → validate the model **in the deployment context** → track intermediate human-machine outcomes (take-up, adherence, access barriers, manipulation) → evaluate development outcomes and cost-effectiveness — is the most directly reusable evaluation frame in the report, and it is not currently held anywhere in this vault as a framework.

**Netherlands childcare-benefits scandal** appears in both Ch.5 and Ch.8: an automated welfare-fraud detection system wrongly flagged **26,000+ individuals** between 2015 and 2019, disproportionately from migrant backgrounds, leading to unemployment, bankruptcies and divorces; the parliamentary inquiry faulted systemic bias and system opacity, and **the prime minister and cabinet resigned in 2021.**

### Ch.6 — Political Impact: the chapter the 2026-08-05 node omitted entirely

This is the most PRIS-relevant chapter outside Ch.9, and none of it was captured. Four axes of power: **between countries; governments ↔ corporations; corporations ↔ individuals; citizens ↔ state.**

**🔷 AI sovereignty as a development trap (pp. 206–207).** Three distinct failure modes, stated as a structured argument:
1. **Measures frequently fail to achieve their goals.** Data localisation is complicated by *"the extraterritorial reach of a cloud provider's home-country laws"*; domestic semiconductor initiatives struggle against incumbent scale economies; *"even countries with a local data center may not be able to avoid upstream dependencies on hardware and software from major foreign providers."*
2. **Domestic political costs.** Sovereignty strategies commit governments to building at home, surfacing siting conflicts — Johor, Malaysia; Brazil; Mexico. *"Unlike a commercial developer that can relocate to another country if blocked in one, a government that has staked a sovereignty claim on building data centers cannot."*
3. **Aggregate cost through fragmentation** — the **"fragmentation doom loop"** (Clayton, Maggiori and Schreger 2025). *"The more countries fragment, the more expensive it is for them to build their own AI stacks… What emerges is a form of a self-reinforcing 'fragmentation doom loop,' which can create a development trap."*

The recommended middle path: diversify suppliers **within** each layer while ensuring interoperability; use open-source and open-weight models; establish strong data governance; **engage in standards setting**; and form coalitions for collective bargaining power. The report is candid that this accepts trade-offs — *"Some choke points will persist."*

Also noted, and worth watching for its rhetorical function: **NVIDIA is named as among the most visible proponents of sovereign-AI narratives**, with Jensen Huang quoted — *"every country needs sovereign AI"* — alongside the observation that foreign technology companies *"benefit greatly from the demand that sovereignty generates."*

**Geopolitical dependency structures.** The **Pax Silica Initiative** (launched by the US in 2025; signatories include Australia, Finland, Greece, India, Israel, Japan, Korea, Norway, Philippines, Qatar, Singapore, Sweden, UAE, UK) is analysed for asymmetric-dependency structure — Figure 6.2 shows signatories overwhelmingly have US defence ties and export dependence, without reciprocal US dependence. **India is a signatory.** The historical parallel drawn is the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety: states with closer EU ties ratified early; those closer to the US ratified late or not at all (Schneider and Urpelainen 2013). *"the mere existence of such asymmetric dependencies can shape the technology choices countries make"* — power need not be exercised or even articulated to operate.

**Lobbying, quantified.** Firms lobbying on AI-related issues in the US **nearly doubled between 2019 and 2025** (Figure 6.3), while immigration lobbying stayed flat. Latin America: **nearly 3,000 documented lobbying actions between 2012 and 2025** (Agência Pública / CLIP investigation). EU: industry engagement is credited with shifting the regulatory posture toward innovation and competitiveness — which contextualises the Digital Omnibus.

**Box 6.1 — the capex comparison worth remembering.** US hyperscaler AI capital spending is projected at **~2% of US GDP in 2026** — more than double the telecom boom's peak share, and about the same as railroads in the 1860s. The 1998 US Commerce Department claim that internet traffic was doubling every 100 days (traced to WorldCom) is set beside the 2018 finding that demand for AI compute doubles every 3.4 months. By 2002 only **2.7% of fibre-optic lines were in use**; 23 telecom firms went bankrupt.

**🔷 Indonesia's Pusat Data Nasional Sementara — a documented AI-infrastructure procurement corruption case.** Officials rigged the tender to benefit Lintasarta, a subsidiary of Indosat Ooredoo Hutchison, in exchange for kickbacks; **estimated state loss 140.8 billion rupiah**; officials were convicted. The report's framing is the useful part: checks held here, but *"if checks and balances fail and transparency is lacking, such conduct may evolve into systemic rent-seeking dynamics"* — with Akcigit, Baslandze and Lotti (2023) on politically connected firms surviving while sectors with more of them grow more slowly. **A concrete, citable instance for the procurement-integrity discussion in Ch.8.**

**🔷 Data externalities — the argument that undercuts consent-based data governance (Kasy 2025).** *"a single user's data are nearly redundant for predicting that user's own behavior, but useful for predicting the behavior of similar users."* The policy implication is stated directly:

> *"Giving individuals property rights over their own data does little to prevent the concentration of AI's predictive capability. Even if many users withheld their data, firms holding the largest existing data sets would retain their advantage. Data externalities of this kind require collective governance arrangements rather than specific provisions of individual contracts between AI providers and users alone."*

**This cuts against the consent architecture of [[06_Frameworks/DPDP_Act_2023]] and against the individual-consent premise of India's DEPA consent-manager model** — which the same chapter otherwise praises as a leading approach. A genuine internal tension in the report, and a strong theoretical hook for P2. Data cooperatives are offered as the collective alternative, with small pilots noted in India and Kenya for gig and informal-sector workers, though *"evidence on their effectiveness at scale is limited."*

**Labour: monopsony and algorithmic management.** Kenyan data labellers paid **$1.32–$2.00 per hour** against a statutory minimum of ~$1.20, for psychologically demanding content review. The 2023 lawsuit by Kenyan content moderators against **Meta and Sama** produced diagnoses of PTSD, depression and anxiety. **Fairwork Cloudwork Ratings found none of 16 platforms reviewed met basic standards** on pay, conditions, contracts, management or worker representation. Box 6.2 covers algorithmic management at Uber (~9 million drivers) and the judicial pushback: the **UK Supreme Court (2021)** classifying drivers as workers; a **Dutch court (2021)** describing algorithmic management as *"a modern relationship of authority"*; **Spain's Rider Law**. The report's distinction is careful and worth preserving: *"algorithmic management is not inherently worse than human management… The concern is not the algorithm itself but the conditions under which it operates."*

**Surveillance.** Beraja, Kao, Yang and Yuchtman (2023), *"AI-tocracy"*: autocracies and weak democracies are **more likely to import facial-recognition technology following domestic political unrest**, and those imports **suppress subsequent unrest**. Carnegie's AI Global Surveillance Index recorded **75+ countries using AI surveillance as of 2019** — described by the report as a lower bound. Function creep is illustrated with Israel's repurposing of counter-terrorism phone tracking for COVID-19 contact tracing. The counterweight case is **Ukraine's DOZORRO**, applying machine learning to the ProZorro e-procurement system to flag irregularities and route citizen complaints to law enforcement.

**🔷 Homogenisation of public discourse — a governance concern distinct from disinformation.** Exposure to opinionated language models shifts users' expressed views (Jakesch et al. 2023); groups writing with a shared model produce less diverse text (Anderson et al. 2024; Padmakumar and He 2024); creative output flattens similarly (Doshi and Hauser 2024). The report's inference:

> *"In such settings, homogenization is a risk distinct from disinformation, and addressing it points toward **supporting a plurality of models so that no single foreign-trained system becomes the default voice in a national public sphere**."*

**This is a public-sphere argument for model plurality, not a competition argument** — and it supplies a rationale for sovereign or regional language models that is independent of the industrial-policy case the report elsewhere treats sceptically. Useful precisely because it cuts across the report's own sovereignty scepticism.

**Fact-checking capacity asymmetry (2025):** Africa had **66 active fact-checking organisations serving 1.5 billion people**; Europe had **139 serving 750 million**. Content moderation covers only a fraction of the world's ~7,000 languages.

### Ch.7 — Governments as Enablers: the policy matrix

**Table 7.2** organises policy by pathway (adopt / adapt / advance) against seven domains — infrastructure, data, compute, business environment, innovation ecosystem, foundational skills, advanced skills. Two structural points the report makes explicitly:
- **Compute, advanced skills, and the innovation ecosystem are absent at the adopt stage** — *"firms using prebuilt AI tools do not yet need local compute, AI engineers, or a frontier research and development ecosystem."* Investment in these before adaptation is premature.
- **Business-environment reform sits entirely at the adopt stage** because the frictions it removes are identical regardless of pathway.

**Box 7.2 — competition policy.** The EU **Digital Markets Act** is cited for requiring gatekeeper interoperability and limiting cross-platform data combination. The report flags a specific developing-country concern: **acquisition of local AI start-ups by foreign incumbents**, which *"can absorb the most promising domestic capabilities before they have had the chance to mature"* — while acknowledging that acquisition prospects also incentivise start-up formation.

**🔷 Box 7.4 — AI infrastructure as a conflict target.** Two datapoints the vault does not hold:
- **Ukraine relocated core public-sector data to overseas cloud providers in the first days of the invasion**, as strikes disrupted data centres, internet exchange points and power supply. More than 100 state registries migrated after parliament reversed a cloud-storage prohibition in 2022.
- **In March 2026, Iranian drone strikes caused structural damage to hyperscale cloud facilities in the Gulf**, prompting insurers, governments and investors to reassess **war-exclusion gaps in standard infrastructure coverage.**

War and civil disturbance accounted for **75% of multilateral political risk insurance issued in fragile and conflict-affected situations between 2010 and 2021.** **This materially changes the risk profile of the data-centre sovereignty argument** and connects to [[03_Domains/Power_Grid_Critical_Infrastructure_Governance]].

**Box 7.1 — the AI gender divide.** Generative AI adoption ~**16% higher among men** across 76 studies in 100+ countries. Across 88 countries, **female-dominated occupations are five times more likely to be automated by generative AI than male-dominated ones (16% vs 3%)** — driven by concentration in clerical and administrative roles (ILO 2026). Credit-scoring models trained on fintech data discriminate against women (Kelley et al. 2022); facial recognition misclassified darker-skinned women at up to **34.7%** against **0.8%** for lighter-skinned men (Buolamwini and Gebru 2018). Algorithmic audits work: **providers publicly named for bias substantially reduced errors within months; unaudited competitors did not** (Raji and Buolamwini 2019). **That last finding is direct empirical support for the audit-mandate position in [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Standard/Assurance_Reuse_Gap]].**

**Low-resource language data (Box 7.3).** Most African languages have **fewer than 10 hours of clearly licensed usable audio.** Malawi's National Language Data Trust — a World Bank Development Data Partnership project with the Malawi Research and Education Network — used content licensing agreements with media houses and government agencies to expand available Chichewa audio **from ~35 hours to more than 10,000 hours at an estimated cost under $10 per hour** across audio, text and video. **A replicable, costed model** with a commercial subscription pathway ensuring creator compensation.

### Spotlights 1, 3, 4 — findings with governance consequences

**Spotlight 1 — Small AI.** The operative sizing table: models under **10 billion parameters** (2–20 GB) run on a phone or laptop with **no connectivity**; 10–100 billion run on a single server over a local network; above 100 billion require a data centre and constant internet. Cost trajectory: the smallest model matching original-ChatGPT capability fell from **540 billion parameters in 2022 to 3.8 billion in 2024**, with output price falling from ~$20 to a few cents per million tokens. Cases: **Nuru** (offline crop-disease diagnosis, outperforming extension officers); **Cough Against TB** (Wadhwani AI with India's Central TB Division, correctly flagging 9 in 10); **PROMPTS** (Kenya, UlizaLlama Swahili model, 3m+ enrolled); **Rori** (Ghana, ~one year of mathematics learning at **~$5 per student**); **Ask Viamo Anything** (voice, basic phones, no literacy required). The market-failure argument is explicit: *"a tool serving a low-income population may never repay its cost, however cheap it is to run, because the people it helps cannot pay for the value it creates."*

**🔷 Spotlight 3 — learning erosion, with the strongest causal evidence in the report.** Strömberg, Lei and Wu (2026), grades 7–12 in China, self-reported AI use rising from near-zero to ~80% over 2022–2025:

| Outcome | Effect |
|---|---|
| Homework scores | **+18%** (with much less time spent) |
| Monthly in-class exam scores | **−20%** |
| Senior high school and college entrance exams, students who had ever used AI | **−5 to −7%**, compounding with length of exposure |

Students who continued spending similar time on homework saw **less** exam impact — effort, not access, is the mediator. Ghana: within months of ChatGPT's launch, a detection tool flagged **60% of teaching-fellowship application essays** as AI-generated; reviewers still rewarded quality but **reduced the "quality bonus" when they suspected AI** (Awuah et al. 2025) — a documented **erosion of credential signalling value**. Acemoglu, Kong and Ozdaglar (2026) supply the theoretical frame: **"knowledge collapse"** — if AI produces context-specific advice accurate enough for most purposes, individuals stop investing in learning, and the *general* knowledge that emerges as a by-product of that learning — a public good — accumulates more slowly. The countervailing evidence is that **design matters**: Chung et al. (2026) found a guardrailed, adaptively sequenced tutor produced substantial gains, particularly among disadvantaged students, while Bastani et al. (2025) found unrestricted access left students *worse off than those who never had access* once it was withdrawn.

**Spotlight 4 — environment.** GenAI e-waste projected from **1.2 million to 5.0 million tons, 2020–2030** (>6% of global e-waste); only 22% of e-waste was recycled in 2022, 12% in Asia, **1% in Africa**; **65% of cross-border e-waste flows from high-income to low- and middle-income countries.** Data-centre electricity consumption grew **~12% per year 2017–2024**, more than four times faster than all other sectors; projected to more than double by 2030. **72% of China's computing capacity sits in regions of severe water scarcity (2022); about two-thirds of US data centres built since 2022 are in high-water-stress areas.** Global data-centre water consumption is projected to rise from ~560 billion to ~1,200 billion litres per year by 2030. On cost incidence, Martin and Peskoe (2025): households sharing a grid with data centres bear a substantial share of the cost of serving them, through concessional pricing to the centres and opaque allocation of infrastructure investment. **Directly relevant to CEA_3 and the power-sector nodes.**

### Part 2 — economic findings with India specificity

**World Bank Enterprise Survey on AI Adoption** (conducted for this Report; formal firms with 5+ employees): India **1,355 firms**, Jordan 358, Kenya 360, Mexico 603, Nigeria 777, Thailand 360, United States 392. Microdata at enterprisesurveys.org.

| Finding | Value |
|---|---|
| Small firms (5–19 workers) using AI chatbots, six developing countries | **~20%** (vs 34% US) |
| Firms in Kenya and Nigeria using AI, 2022 → 2025 | **<5% → ~40%**, matching US rates |
| Gap in **digital readiness**, small firms (US vs developing) | **6 pp** |
| Gap in **AI sophistication**, medium and large firms | **48 pp** |
| ChatGPT adoption, share of internet users (April 2025) | HIC **~25%** · UMIC **5.8%** · LMIC **4.7%** · **LIC 0.7%** |

**The structural point:** adoption gaps are modest and closing; **sophistication gaps are large and widening.** Shallow adoption without depth is the pattern — *"AI use is often shallow, concentrated on information searches, writing, and translation rather than deeper changes in how businesses and governments operate."*

**India labour-market evidence:** generative AI reduced monthly job postings for the most AI-exposed white-collar occupations by roughly **20%**, with disproportionate impact on entry-level workers (World Bank 2025c, *South Asia Development Update*). Box 4.3 shows South Asian firms' postings declined **1.6%** post-ChatGPT overall, with larger declines among **MNC affiliates and global value chain suppliers** than local firms.

**Growth projections (Kose and Stamm 2026):** AI could raise average annual potential growth during the 2020s from **1.2% to 3.6%** in advanced economies under the most optimistic scenario, but only from **4.1% to 4.9%** in EMDEs. **About two-thirds of the productivity gap is attributable to lower adoption rates, not lower exposure** — which locates the policy lever squarely on adoption rather than on task composition.

## Analytical Significance for PRIS Research

### For P1 (AI Governance SLR)
Contributes a citable, World Bank-endorsed Enabler-User-Regulator typology and an explicit "regulatory fragmentation" framing that extends this vault's existing OECD/NIST/EU AI Act comparator set with a developing-country-capacity lens none of those three carry. The report's own confirmed position — voluntary standards before binding law, sequenced via a life-cycle model — is a fourth data point for comparative regulatory-philosophy classification alongside EU (ex-ante, risk-tiered), NIST (iterative, voluntary profile-based), and OECD (principle-based, non-binding).

### For fsQCA_Thesis_Chapter — candidate configurational conditions
The Enabler/User/Regulator role-presence and the Spotlight 7 horizontal/vertical coordination-layer presence/absence are both plausible calibratable conditions. The EU's now-delayed Annex III timeline versus jurisdictions moving to mandatory obligations gives a live natural-experiment outcome variable worth tracking for the truth table.

### For P2 (DPI Governance) — Records_Rules_Asymmetry, second external corroboration
Spotlight 2/5's algorithmic public-service-delivery framing and the now-verified Bharat-VISTAAR case give P2 a second, dated, India-specific empirical touchpoint (after [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_25_World_Bank_DPI_Development_2025]]'s "build legal framework in parallel with rollout" lesson) from the same issuing institution — useful as convergent World Bank evidence rather than a single-source claim.

### 🔷 New, 2026-08-09 — five contributions the primary text unlocks

**1. Assurance reuse is a divergence, not a deficit.** Colorado and Texas grant liability safe harbour for NIST AI RMF compliance; the EU AI Act grants conformity presumption via harmonised standards; **RBI_14 para 46(i) expressly refuses assurance reuse.** The vault has carried [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Standard/Assurance_Reuse_Gap]] as an absence inferred from regulatory silence. It can now be stated as a **positional difference between jurisdictions** — which is a stronger and more publishable claim, because it has a comparator rather than a void.

**2. Parallelism has a normative defence.** The **"shared AI governance floor"** formulation gives [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Regulatory_Parallelism]] something it has lacked: a position under which parallel regimes are acceptable provided a common floor of rights protection and interoperability holds. Any P1 or fsQCA treatment that frames parallelism purely as fragmentation now has to engage this.

**3. Bradford's three-model thesis is contested by a World Bank flagship.** Box 9.4 is a positioning asset. Bradford appears nowhere in the vault, which is itself a gap worth closing — the comparative-regulation literature that P1 sits in largely runs through *Digital Empires*.

**4. The evidence base is quantifiably thin.** **Seventeen** causally-identified studies of frontline AI health interventions in low-resource contexts, out of ~9,762. Plus Bangladesh's phone-based targeting underperforming proxy-means testing. Together these justify an evidence-gap claim in P1 without hedging.

**5. Standard-setting participation is quantified.** Figure 9.6 (118 countries party to none of seven initiatives; 48 of 54 African Group) and the one-third developing-country share of ISO/IEC AI committees give the vault hard numbers where it previously had qualitative assertions. **Feeds ⚠#304** at [[06_Frameworks/ISO_IEC_42001]].

### Ch.8 as a governance frame — the project/product distinction

**Box 8.3's project-versus-product argument has no equivalent in the vault** and deserves treatment as a framework rather than a passing citation. It reframes [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Governance_Debt]] in budgetary terms: funding AI as a fixed-duration capital project, with operations and maintenance underfunded at exactly the point where governance requirements peak, produces predictable degradation and a repeated modernisation cycle at rising cost. Combined with the pre-contractual lock-in observation — that pilots and donor-funded packages effectively select the vendor before procurement rules engage — this is directly applicable to **donor-financed AI in the DPI space**, which is P2's territory.

### Coordination/Parallelism pattern — relationship to [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Regulatory_Parallelism]] and [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Institutional_Coherence]]
Spotlight 7's fragmentation problem is this vault's [[Regulatory_Parallelism]] construct observed at the *international/comparative* scale rather than the intra-jurisdiction multi-regulator scale P3's BFSI work documents for India. The two-layer coordination model is offered by the report as a remedy — i.e., an explicit [[Institutional_Coherence]]-building mechanism. Worth a cross-reference note in both concept files: this is the vault's first non-India, non-BFSI instance of the parallelism/coherence pair operating at the level of *national AI-policy architecture* rather than sectoral regulator overlap.
