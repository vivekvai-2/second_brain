## Substantive Content

### Document Structure

Introduction → Ch.1 "The Need for Responsible AI" (Ethical, Systems, and Societal Considerations) → Ch.2 "Legal and Regulatory Approaches for Managing AI Systems" → Ch.3 "Technology Based Approach for Managing AI Systems" → Ch.4 "Principles for Responsible Management of AI Systems" → Appendices: (1) Self-Assessment Guide for AI Usage, (2) Review of Global Regulatory Landscape, (3) Model Transparency Mechanisms.

### The Seven Principles (Box 13, verbatim)

1. **Principle of Safety and Reliability** — "AI should be deployed reliably as intended and sufficient safeguards must be placed to ensure the safety of relevant stakeholders... The AI system needs to be monitored through its lifecycle so it performs in an acceptable manner, reliably, according to the desired goals."
2. **Principle of Equality** — "AI systems must treat individuals under same circumstances relevant to the decision equally."
3. **Principle of Inclusivity and Non-discrimination** — "AI systems should not deny opportunity to a qualified person on the basis of their identity. It should not deepen the harmful historic and social divisions based on religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth or residence..."
4. **Principle of Privacy and Security** — "AI should maintain privacy and security of data of individuals or entities that is used for training the system. Access should be provided only to those authorized with sufficient safeguards."
5. **Principle of Transparency** — "The design and functioning of the AI system should be recorded and made available for external scrutiny and audit..."
6. **Principle of Accountability** — "All stakeholders involved in the design, development and deployment of the AI system must be responsible for their actions... conduct risk and impact assessments... set up an auditing process... create mechanisms for grievance redressal..."
7. **Principle of protection and reinforcement of positive human values** — "AI should promote positive human values and not disturb in any way social harmony in community relationships."

**Notable grounding:** the Principles are explicitly derived from the Constitution of India and constitutional-morality jurisprudence, citing *Naz Foundation* and *Navtej Johar* — a distinctive India-specific normative anchor not present in most international AI-principles frameworks (contrast OECD/EU's grounding in secular human-rights instruments).

### Structural/Institutional Recommendations

- Explicitly states India "does not have an overarching guidance framework for the use of AI systems" and calls for establishing one
- Recommends a **graded, risk-based, sector-specific regulatory approach** rather than one-size-fits-all — "balancing soft governance measures with regulation depending on the use case and risks involved"
- Flags the forthcoming Part 2 (NITI_3) to cover implementation/enforcement mechanisms across public sector, private sector, and academia
- Appendix 1 offers a practical Self-Assessment Guide (ethics committees, grievance redressal mechanisms) for AI deployers
- Notes existing but incomplete building blocks as of Feb 2021: the draft PDP Bill 2019 (predecessor to the eventual DPDP Act 2023 — see [[06_Frameworks/DPDP_Act_2023]]), IT Act 2000 + SPDI Rules 2011, SEBI AI/ML reporting circulars (2019), NDHM strategy, and NITI's own DEPA framework

## Analytical Significance for PRIS Research

### For P1 (AI Governance SLR)

**Textual comparison completed (2026-07-31):** Direct comparison against [[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_FREE_AI_2025]]'s "Seven Sutras" (Trust, People First, Innovation over Restraint, Fairness & Equity, Accountability, Understandable by Design, Safety/Resilience/Sustainability) and [[07_Institutions/Corpus/MeitY/MeitY_2]]'s explicitly-adapted version of the same list shows substantial normative overlap with NITI_1's Seven Principles (both sets converge on safety, fairness/equality, transparency/understandability, and accountability as core pillars) but **zero cross-citation in either direction** — neither RBI_FREE_AI_2025 nor MeitY_2 references NITI_1 or NITI_3 anywhere in their extracted text, despite a four-year gap (2021 vs. 2025) and NITI Aayog's institutional standing as India's premier policy think tank. India's AI governance principles have therefore not "drifted" so much as been **independently re-derived twice**: once by NITI Aayog in 2021 from constitutional-morality jurisprudence (*Naz Foundation*, *Navtej Johar*), and again by RBI in 2025 from sector-specific financial-stability reasoning, with MeitY_2 subsequently diffusing RBI's version horizontally across government (an explicit, named convergence). The NITI↔RBI/MeitY relationship itself, by contrast, fits [[Regulatory_Parallelism]]'s definition — equivalent content, no coordination mechanism — logged as a new instance there (2026-07-31 entry). This is a citable finding for P1 (AI Governance SLR): India's principle-level AI governance discourse shows within-lineage convergence (RBI→MeitY) but cross-lineage parallelism (NITI vs. RBI/MeitY), suggesting institutional silos operate even at the level of foundational values, not just operational rules.

### Constitutional grounding as a distinctive feature

The explicit citation of *Naz Foundation* and *Navtej Johar* (both landmark Article 21/privacy/dignity jurisprudence) to ground AI ethics principles is a genuinely distinctive move worth flagging for P4's doctrinal analysis — India is one of relatively few jurisdictions grounding AI principles in domestic constitutional case law rather than purely in international soft-law instruments.

### Coordination / Sequencing Pattern

NITI_1 (Feb 2021, principles) → NITI_3 (Aug 2021, operationalization) is a clean intra-institution sequential pattern, six months apart — the shortest such gap in the NITI Aayog corpus, suggesting this was a planned two-part release rather than an ad hoc follow-up.
