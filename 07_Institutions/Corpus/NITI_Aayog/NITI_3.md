## Substantive Content

### Seven Principles for Responsible AI (Confirmed in Part 2)

| Principle | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Safety and Reliability | AI deployed reliably as intended; sufficient safeguards for relevant stakeholders |
| Equality | AI systems treat individuals under same circumstances equally |
| Inclusivity and Non-discrimination | AI must not deny opportunity on identity grounds; must not deepen historical divisions |
| Privacy and Security | AI must maintain privacy/security of training data; access only to authorised parties |
| Transparency | Design and functioning recorded and available for external scrutiny and audit |
| Accountability | All stakeholders in design, development, deployment must be responsible for actions |
| Protection and reinforcement of positive human values | AI must promote positive human values; not disturb social harmony |

### Governance Architecture — Role of Government (Chapter 3)

**Four Areas of Government Intervention:**
1. Regulatory Interventions (risk-based approach)
2. Policy Making
3. Awareness and Capacity Building
4. Procurement

**Risk-Based Regulatory Mechanism:**
- Greater potential for harm → more stringent requirements
- Low-risk: Self-regulation + awareness campaigns
- Unknown-risk: Policy sandboxes and controlled deployments
- High-risk (fundamental rights violation likelihood): Mandatory responsible AI practices
- Constitutional test: Potential to violate fundamental rights = highest scrutiny

**Council for Ethics and Technology (CET) — Finalised Design:**
- Independent, multi-disciplinary, apex-level advisory body
- Composition: CS/AI experts, legal experts, sectoral experts, civil society, humanities/social science, private sector/industry, environmental expert, national security expert, cybersecurity expert, standards bodies
- Sub-groups for emerging technologies and individual sectors
- Functions: Policy harmonisation; model procurement documents; capacity building (with DoPT, MeitY, NITI Aayog); knowledge hub on AI policy

**Key CET mandates:**
- Drive convergence across sectoral regulators — prevent duplication and inconsistent policies
- Leverage learnings from SEBI AI governance, ICMR, NDHM as model guidelines
- State-level considerations: Telangana AI framework and Tamil Nadu Safe/Ethical AI Policy cited as precedents

### Government Procurement Framework (§3.42–3.45)
- Ethical advisory committee for complex AI procurement
- Responsible AI principles must be embedded in procurement documents
- Liability for AI violations to be addressed in procurement documentation
- Model RFP documents needed

### Private Sector Mechanisms (Chapter 4)

**Compliance Mechanisms:**
- Assign accountability for responsible AI to leadership team member
- Leverage open courses, workshops, open materials
- Internal ethics boards, self-assessment guides, external audits
- Multi-disciplinary stakeholder engagement

**High-Risk AI Mandate:**
- Identification of high-risk use cases by CET in consultation with sectoral authorities
- Creates compliance demand signal for Indian AI ecosystem

### Research and Academic Institutions (§4.2)
- Foundational AI ethics courses at secondary/senior secondary level
- Graduate programs: identify and anticipate ethical issues; address social considerations
- SWAYAM/NPTEL for cross-disciplinary accessible curriculum
- IRB augmentation with AI ethics expertise

### Appendix 1 — Example Responsible AI Frameworks
- Tamil Nadu DEEP-MAX Scorecard (TAM-DEF Framework)
- IEEE P7000 (Model process for addressing ethical concerns during system design)
- WEF Oversight Toolkit for Boards
- Chatbots RESET (WEF — responsible conversational AI in healthcare)

### Appendix 2 — Global Risk-Based Approaches
- Germany: Criticality pyramid + risk-adapted regulatory system
- EU AI Act (April 2021 proposal): Unacceptable / High / Low or minimal risk
- US FDA: AI/ML-Based Software as Medical Device Action Plan
- Australia: AI Ethics Framework (probability × consequence risk matrix)

## Analytical Significance for PRIS Research

### Foundational Document for India AI Governance Case (fsQCA)
- Defines India's governance configuration: Advisory body (non-mandatory) + risk-based intervention + self-regulation as default
- Explicitly acknowledges that sectoral regulators (SEBI, RBI, IRDAI) continue to govern AI in their domains
- The CET proposal was the proposed coordination mechanism to prevent regulatory parallelism — its non-implementation is the key governance gap
- India coding in fsQCA: Soft governance configuration (advisory + self-regulation) = consistent with this document's framework

### Coordination Problem Explicitly Acknowledged
- §3.57: CET tasked with driving convergence across stakeholders including SEBI, ICMR, NDHM — the exact parallelism that P3 documents in BFSI
- NITI Aayog recognised in 2021 that separate AI governance approaches were emerging without coordination

### Governance Debt Timeline Evidence
- CET proposed August 2021; not established as of June 2026 — 5-year governance debt accumulation
- No coordination body between SEBI, RBI, CERT-In, MeitY, IRDAI on AI governance has been established
- This is the definitional governance gap driving the AI Governance Wiki and P3 paper

### Constitutional Anchoring (P4 / IJLIT)
- Responsible AI principles explicitly derived from Constitution of India
- Supreme Court's "Constitutional morality" standard cited as benchmark
- AI accountability framework in India is constitutionally grounded — important for doctrinal legal analysis in P4
