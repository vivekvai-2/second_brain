# **DPI Governance**

**Type:** Domain**Status:** Active**Last Updated:** 2026-06-11

---

## **Core Synthesis**

DPI Governance is examined in this research as a question of how enforceable obligations are allocated across participants within Digital Public Infrastructure systems rather than how digital infrastructures are technically designed. The domain focuses on the institutional arrangements, governance instruments, and accountability structures governing welfare delivery and financial infrastructure systems in India. Analysis of four DPI cases reveals a recurring structural pattern in which citizens are extensively represented within governance architectures as records but rarely as rule-bearing parties within enforceable obligations. This asymmetry generates a governance architecture that is highly attentive to system performance and infrastructure reliability while remaining comparatively silent on citizen-facing fallback mechanisms. The central contribution of this domain is therefore not an evaluation of DPI effectiveness, but a diagnosis of how governance architectures distribute formal obligations across sociotechnical actors.

---

## **Scope and Boundary**

DPI Governance concerns the institutional arrangements, regulatory instruments, and accountability structures through which Digital Public Infrastructure systems in India are governed. The domain includes welfare and financial infrastructure systems such as Aadhaar, PM-KISAN, Account Aggregator, and PM-JAY, with particular emphasis on how governance rules allocate responsibility among architects, intermediaries, institutions, and citizens. The focus is on governance as an infrastructural phenomenon rather than a technical architecture problem. Development economics, welfare outcomes, and engineering design questions fall outside the analytical boundary unless they are directly connected to the allocation of enforceable obligations within governance instruments.

---

## **Key Debates**

### **Records vs Rules Asymmetry**

The central debate concerns the distinction between being represented within a governance system and being represented within its enforceable rules. Across the coded corpus, citizens appear extensively as data subjects, beneficiaries, identity holders, and transaction participants. However, this visibility as records does not translate into equivalent visibility as rule-bearing actors. The empirical finding that none of the eleven Rules identified in the corpus govern citizen-facing fallback procedures, language provisions, or intermediary accountability suggests a structural asymmetry rather than an isolated omission. The research therefore argues that DPI governance architectures may successfully inscribe citizens into records while simultaneously excluding them from the enforceable rule structures that govern system failure and remediation.

### **Technical-Citizen Rule Allocation**

Governance obligations within DPI systems are concentrated around technical performance, operational reliability, verification processes, and institutional compliance. Citizen-facing obligations and protections are comparatively weakly formalised and sparsely sanctioned. This allocation pattern creates an uneven distribution of governance attention in which technical actors and infrastructures become the primary subjects of regulation. The resulting architecture affects how responsibility is assigned when failures occur because procedural remedies are often less developed than technical controls. The asymmetry therefore represents a governance design choice rather than a simple implementation gap.

### **Infrastructure Governance vs Service Governance**

The empirical evidence suggests that DPI systems are governed primarily as infrastructures rather than as services. Infrastructure governance prioritises reliability, interoperability, standardisation, scalability, and operational performance. Service governance would additionally require formal attention to user remedies, fallback pathways, accessibility, and intermediary accountability. The coded corpus demonstrates that governance instruments overwhelmingly privilege the former. This distinction constitutes the primary contribution of P2 because it identifies a structural gap that conventional discussions of DPI success, adoption, and scale do not readily detect.

### **IS Framing vs Public Administration Framing**

The structural absence identified in this research becomes visible only when DPI is examined through Information Systems theory. Public administration approaches are effective in evaluating policy design, administrative burden, and welfare outcomes, but they are less equipped to analyse how participants are inscribed into governance architectures through data structures and rule systems. The Information Systems lens focuses attention on records, infrastructures, digital artefacts, and institutional inscription. This perspective reveals that governance architectures can simultaneously increase informational visibility while limiting formal rule-bearing participation. The domain therefore contributes to ongoing efforts to develop contextually grounded Information Systems theory from India.

## **Empirical Base**

The domain is grounded in a document-based analysis of four DPI governance cases: PM-KISAN eligibility determination, Aadhaar Face Authentication identity verification, Account Aggregator-enabled AI credit assessment, and PM-JAY fraud analytics. The empirical corpus consists of 248 coded institutional statements extracted from twelve governance documents and analysed using Institutional Grammar 2.0 and ADICO syntax.

The corpus reveals a consistent governance pattern across otherwise diverse positions within the algorithmic decision chain. Governance instruments are dense in system-facing obligations but comparatively thin in citizen-facing obligations. Of the eleven Rules identified in the corpus, none govern citizen-facing fallback procedures, language provisions, or intermediary accountability. The pattern is reinforced by the directional allocation of procedural consequences. In three of the four cases examined, failures in the system ultimately impose procedural burdens on beneficiaries rather than on the infrastructure itself. Only PM-JAY exhibits a different allocation pattern, with procedural consequences falling primarily on hospitals. Taken together, these findings support the argument that the observed asymmetry is structural rather than incidental.

## **Dominant Theoretical Frames**

**Data-as-records lineage** — Kallinikos, Aaltonen, and Marton (2013), together with subsequent work by Alaimo and Kallinikos, provide the primary Information Systems grammar through which citizens are analysed as records within digital infrastructures.

**IS digital infrastructure theory** — Tilson, Lyytinen, and Sørensen (2010) and Henfridsson and Bygstad (2013) explain how infrastructures inscribe participants and distribute capabilities and constraints across sociotechnical systems.

**Institutional Grammar 2.0** — ADICO coding provides the methodological foundation for identifying patterns in the allocation of institutional obligations across governance documents.

**Contextual theorisation** — Avgerou (2019) provides the justification for treating India not merely as an empirical setting but as a theoretically significant context for Information Systems research.

## **Open Research Questions**

**OQ-DPI-01:** Does the records-rules asymmetry pattern hold beyond welfare delivery into financial infrastructure, ONDC, and urban infrastructure governance systems?

**OQ-DPI-02:** What governance design interventions would enable citizens to become rule-bearing parties without undermining infrastructure scalability and operational efficiency?

**OQ-DPI-03:** How do DPI governance architectures across India, Brazil, and Kenya compare when analysed through a records-rules diagnostic?

**OQ-DPI-04:** Can records-rules asymmetry be operationalised as a governance audit instrument for DPI architects, regulators, and policymakers?

### Confirmed Gaps and Findings (2026-08-02 Perplexity Deep Research)

- **No fsQCA-methodology paper applied to DPI governance exists** — a confirmed open research niche (see [[International_25_World_Bank_DPI_Development_2025]] for the fuller argument) and a genuine white-space opportunity to extend this domain's existing 12-document/248-statement IG2.0 corpus into a small-N fsQCA design.
- **No MOSIP-self-published AI-governance document exists** — MOSIP appears only as a subject within third-party publications (World Bank, IIM Bangalore), never as an issuing institution in its own right.
- **AI-DPI intersection language is emerging but thin.** The Account Aggregator ecosystem is officially described (PIB, 19 November 2025) as "strengthening the digital credit infrastructure and enhancing the effectiveness of AI-based credit models" — direct GoI confirmation that DPI increasingly underwrites AI-based financial decisioning, reinforcing this domain's relevance to P3/BFSI work even though its primary framing remains welfare delivery.
- **UIDAI has no AI-specific circular** despite confirming (via its own official channel, not a formal circular) that its Face Authentication system is AI/ML-based — this is a documented instance of the domain's own "records vs rules asymmetry" argument at the security-governance layer: the technology has moved to AI/ML, the governing instrument has not followed.
- **Vidhi's N.U.D.G.E. proposal** (a dedicated User-Driven Governance Entity for the Account Aggregator ecosystem, 7 June 2023) is a directly relevant existing proposal for exactly the kind of citizen-facing governance mechanism this domain's Records_Rules_Asymmetry argument identifies as missing — worth citing as evidence that the gap has been named by outside researchers, not only by this corpus.

### DEPA Sourcing Pass — Findings (2026-08-02, second Perplexity run)

The domain's consent layer now has full framework and corpus anchoring: [[06_Frameworks/DEPA]], [[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_11_NBFC_Account_Aggregator_Master_Directions_2016]], [[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_12_ReBIT_AA_API_Specifications_2023]], [[07_Institutions/Corpus/Sahamati/Sahamati_1_SRO_Recognition_and_Grievance_Architecture_2026]], plus new institution notes [[Sahamati]] and [[NPCI]].

- **New pattern — "operationalisation without enactment."** DEPA was published as a *Draft for Discussion* (NITI Aayog, Aug 2020), never finalised or superseded, yet became operative in two sectors and was subsequently written into statute via DPDP Rules 2025 Rule 4. The RBI Master Directions implementing it **predate the document by four years**. This is the mirror image of DPDP's "enacted but not operative" and gives the domain a two-sided account of instrument-status/reality divergence. Full treatment in [[06_Frameworks/DEPA]].
- **The N.U.D.G.E. gap has been half-closed, in the wrong direction.** RBI recognised [[Sahamati]] as SRO for the AA ecosystem on 5 June 2026 — a governance entity now exists, but it is an **industry** body, not the **user-driven** entity Vidhi proposed. The Records_Rules_Asymmetry argument survives the development and is arguably strengthened: when a governance layer was finally built, it was built for participants rather than for data principals.
- **Coherence counter-case.** RBI, SEBI, IRDAI and PFRDA co-authored DEPA and their four ombudsman schemes jointly form tier 3 of the AA grievance chain — the clearest operational instance of cross-regulator coordination in the corpus. The same four regulators produce non-cross-referencing AI instruments. Same institutions, same period, coherent on consent and parallel on AI: this isolates the parallelism variable to subject matter rather than institutional capacity, and should be written into [[Regulatory_Parallelism]] as a boundary condition.
- **NPCI: deontic placement, not governance absence** *(restated 2026-08-15 — the earlier "federated AI fraud detection" framing was refuted by primary sourcing; see ⚠#361).* NPCI's **EFRM** issues ecosystem-level fraud alerts that member banks are directed to act on with priority — a decision input crossing an institutional boundary. NPCI is an authorised non-bank **PSO, not a Regulated Entity**, so RBI's AI instruments do not reach it and the 2021 Digital Payment Security Controls Directions do not apply. **One** instrument does reach it — the *Master Directions on Cyber Resilience and Digital Payment Security Controls for non-bank PSOs* (`RBI/DPSS/2024-25/123`, Large Non-Bank PSO, from 1 Apr 2025) — **and it contains no AI provision.** Same regulator, same statutory power, same entity: obligations imposed for cyber, withheld for AI. A [[Governance_Debt]] instance at the infrastructure rather than regulator layer. See [[07_Institutions/Corpus/NPCI/NPCI_1_AI_ML_Payments_Operations_2025_2026]].
- **Telecom DEPA extension: confirmed absent.** The 2020 draft claimed telecom piloting was underway; no DoT, TRAI or NITI instrument operationalising it was located. Independently corroborates [[Telecom_AI_Governance]].
- **No cross-sectoral consent layer exists as an operative instrument.** The National Health Stack's "Consent layer" framing is architectural only; health's HIE-CM and finance's AA remain separate instantiations.
- ⚠ **Open conflict:** DEPA's public-comment closing date is given as 1 October 2020 in the Executive Summary and 30 November 2020 in a secondary source. Unresolved — do not cite a closing date.
