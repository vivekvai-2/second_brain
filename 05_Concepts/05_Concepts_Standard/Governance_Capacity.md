# **Governance Capacity**

**Type:** Standard Concept  
**Status:** Active  
**Last Updated:** 2026-06-27 (Batch 15 instances added)  
**Theoretical Lineage:** Regulatory governance; institutional theory; public administration

---

## **Definition**

Governance Capacity is the degree to which regulatory institutions and governed enterprises possess the authority, resources, processes, and technical competence required to implement, monitor, and enforce AI governance obligations effectively. Within this research programme, the construct is distinct from governance design, which concerns the structure of governance requirements, and from governance compliance, which concerns whether those requirements are followed in practice. Governance Capacity operates at both regulatory and enterprise levels, although the capabilities required at each level differ substantially in terms of authority, enforcement powers, organisational processes, and technical infrastructure.

## **Application in This Research**

Within [[fsQCA_Thesis_Chapter]], Governance Capacity underpins the Enforcement Architecture (EA) condition, where jurisdictions are evaluated based on the authority, resources, and demonstrated enforcement capability of their governance institutions. Within [[AI_Governance]], the governance maturity framework operationalises capacity at the organisational level, with most regulated Indian organisations assessed as operating between Levels 2 and 3 while regulators increasingly expect Level 3 capability as a minimum threshold. Within [[P3_BFSI_JEIM]], Governance Capacity is indirectly implicated through the absence of effective coordination between RBI and SEBI, suggesting limitations in cross-regulator governance capability despite strong sectoral authority. Within [[P4_Doctrinal_IJLIT]], Governance Capacity functions as a normative requirement because the proposed redesign of AI governance and Three Lines of Defence structures assumes capabilities that many existing BFSI governance architectures do not yet possess.

## **Governance Maturity Levels**

|**Level**|**Label**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|
|1|Initial|Unpredictable, poorly controlled processes|
|2|Managed|Basic governance artefacts exist (policy, register)|
|3|Defined|Standardised processes; decision authority clear|
|4|Quantitatively Managed|Measured and controlled; metrics track effectiveness|
|5|Optimised|Continuous improvement; proactive adaptation|

## **Boundary Conditions**

- Governance Capacity is a property of institutions, governance systems, and organisational arrangements rather than of individual compliance officers or governance professionals.
- Capacity at the regulator level concerns authority, resources, supervisory capability, and enforcement effectiveness, whereas capacity at the enterprise level concerns governance processes, skills, controls, and supporting infrastructure.
- High Governance Capacity is necessary but not sufficient for legitimate governance outcomes because factors such as [[Institutional_Coherence]] also influence governance performance and legitimacy.

## **Theoretical Positioning**

Governance Capacity is theoretically grounded in regulatory governance scholarship, particularly the capability-based frameworks developed by Lodge and Wegrich (2012), who distinguish between regulatory delivery capacity, coordination capacity, and analytical capacity as distinct institutional properties. Institutional theory contributes through its attention to the organisational fields and resource environments within which regulatory bodies develop or fail to develop effective enforcement capabilities. Within information systems research, governance capacity connects to IT governance maturity models, particularly the CMMI and COBIT lineages, which operationalise capacity as a progression from ad hoc to optimised governance practice. The five-level maturity framework reproduced in this note synthesises these traditions into a diagnostic instrument applicable at both the regulatory and enterprise levels. Within PRIS, the construct performs a bridging function: it connects the structural diagnosis of Regulatory Parallelism and Governance Debt to the organisational reality that reducing those structural burdens requires capacity that many Indian BFSI institutions do not yet possess.

## **Future Reuse Opportunities**

- Develop a Governance Capacity diagnostic instrument for BFSI consulting engagements, mapping regulated entities against the five-level maturity framework and identifying the gap between current capability and regulatory expectation.
- Use the fsQCA Enforcement Architecture (EA) condition calibration to test empirically whether regulatory-level governance capacity is a necessary condition for legitimate AI governance outcomes across the ten-case corpus.
- Incorporate governance capacity assessment into the BFSI AI Governance Handbook as the opening diagnostic chapter, establishing the baseline against which the handbook's implementation roadmap is calibrated.
- Develop comparative research examining how governance capacity at the regulatory level — supervisory staffing, technical expertise, examination capability — shapes the effectiveness of binding AI governance instruments across RBI, SEBI, and international comparators.

Air Canada* (BC Civil Resolution Tribunal, 2024) and *Mata v. Avianca* (SDNY, 2023) establish that governance capacity gaps at the deployer level generate direct legal liability — the case-law anchor for why Maturity Level 2 is insufficient.
- (Batch 15 — European banks op-risk capital modelling gap) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Butler_Brooks_Operational_Risk_Paradigm_Change_2024]] (F049) — deep capacity failure: the ~80% of European banks not modelling their op-risk capital allocation constitutes direct empirical evidence of deep governance incapacity in the operational risk layer. Capacity failure here is not about missing frameworks (the Basel frameworks exist) but about institutionally entrenched inability to execute them substantively.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-15 — TrustScore ladder as portable capacity-calibration instrument) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F075_ResponsibleAITrust_Global_AI_Governance_Alignment_Map]] — Responsible AI Trust's five-level TrustScore ladder (Unverified → Documented → Auditable → Certified → Trusted) and its "portable AI governance file" artifact checklist (risk register, model evaluations, incident log, AI-BOM, provenance documentation) provide a certification-portability framed governance-capacity instrument, distinct from F038's CMMI process-maturity model and F037's four-stage progressive-governance framework — a third maturity-model variant in the corpus, oriented toward cross-jurisdictional demonstrability rather than internal process depth.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-15 — Frontier-vs-median adoption gap as capacity-lag signal) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F077_OpenAI_State_of_Enterprise_AI_2025]] — OpenAI's self-reported finding that "frontier" workers/firms send 6×/2× more messages than the median is, read against governance capacity, a likely leading indicator that fast-adopting business units are outpacing governance maturity — usage-velocity data of this kind is not itself a governance-capacity measurement, but flags where a future capacity gap is most likely to open first. Source is thin (partial extraction, ⚠#217); treat as a directional signal, not a calibrated GC data point.
- (Batch 15 — Responsible AI literacy as capacity prerequisite) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Papagiannidis_Mikalef_Conboy_RAI_Governance_Framework_2025]] (F050) — relational practice: the paper's focus on "responsible AI literacy" frames competency-building explicitly as a capacity prerequisite for effective RAI governance. The paper identifies "what skills and competencies employees at different roles and levels need to be equipped with" as a critical open question — confirming that governance capacity deficits are structurally constitutive of the RAI implementation problem.
- (Batch 15 continuation — AIIRS institutional capacity requirement) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/RAND_GovAI_AI_Incident_Reporting_Institutional_Design]] (F051) — Wei/Anderljung/Shevlane/Siegmann seven-dimension AIIRS framework demonstrates that effective incident reporting requires substantial institutional capacity (technical causal analysis, database infrastructure, multi-actor coordination, immunity administration). Low-capacity regulators cannot implement full seven-dimension specification; India's absence of AIIRS is a direct capacity gap. Two-track (mandatory/attributed + voluntary/non-attributed) architecture is capacity-doubling: requires two institutions, not one.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Layer-aware regulatory capacity requirement) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Paladin_GI_AI_Tech_Stack_Primer]] (F052) — Walden & Lynch: layer-aware AI regulation requires technical capacity to assess compute, model, and application layer risks independently. Capacity-poor regulators default to application-layer-only governance; India's regulatory architecture operates predominantly at Layer 3 (MeitY 2024 advisory) with no Layer 2 (frontier model evaluation, model registry) instrument. Compute layer (Layer 1) is addressed via industrial policy (IndiaAI/SemiconIndia) without governance integration — a capacity segmentation pattern.
- (Batch 15 continuation — FRIA as capacity-building instrument) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/FRA_EU_Assessing_High_Risk_AI_Fundamental_Rights]] (F053) — FRA's step-by-step FRIA methodology (six-step process, eight rights domains) is explicitly designed as a capacity-building instrument for public sector deployers who may lack in-house legal or technical capacity to conduct fundamental rights assessments unaided. SME exemption from mandatory FRIA is a capacity-recognition mechanism. India has no comparable capacity-building instrument for AI impact assessment.
- (Batch 15 continuation — BFSI-industry capacity architecture) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/MindForge_MAS_AI_Risk_Management_Executive_Handbook]] (F054) — MindForge's Consideration C15 (Talent and Capability) explicitly addresses governance capacity: Board and senior management AI literacy, specialist AI risk function, workforce upskilling. Board AI literacy is framed as a capacity threshold — governance capacity is not merely institutional infrastructure but distributed cognitive capacity across the governance chain. India RBI/SEBI have no equivalent Board AI literacy expectation.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Practitioner-level capacity taxonomy) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/IAPP_AIGP_Body_of_Knowledge_v21_2026]] (F055) — IAPP AIGP BoK v2.1 defines what practitioner-level governance capacity means across four domains (Foundations, Laws/Standards, Development, Deployment). A jurisdiction whose regulatory and institutional staff cannot pass a Domain I-IV competency assessment is governance-capacity constrained by definition. India shows Domain III/IV (development and deployment governance) as minimal — the most actionable practitioner-competency deficit.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Government-as-deployer capacity-building) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_20_Australia_DTA_AI_Impact_Assessment_Tool]] — DTA AIIA Tool imposes mandatory pre-deployment assessment on Commonwealth entities, requiring agency capacity to complete 12-section workbook. DTA guidance materials are explicit capacity-building instruments; SES-Band elevated approval chain for High risk systems creates capacity-graded institutional response. India has no whole-of-government AI impact assessment capacity architecture.
- (Batch 18 — Control-catalogue as checklist-style GC complement) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F070_Databricks_AI_Security_Framework_DASF]] — Databricks DASF's 51 numbered mitigation controls (DASF 1–51), each tagged against a 6-way deployment-model applicability matrix, give a concrete control-inventory instrument that complements F038's ordinal 5-level maturity model: F038 measures *how mature* an organisation's agentic AI security governance is; F070 measures *which specific controls* are or are not in place. External validation from Capital One and Navy Federal Credit Union (both regulated US financial institutions) gives this vendor framework unusual BFSI-sector relevance. Most Indian BFSI entities are estimated to have implemented well under half of the 51 controls, placing them at a low position on this checklist-style capacity measure — vendor self-interest in Databricks-native tooling recommendations should be discounted when using this as a capacity benchmark.
- (Batch 18 — Phased governance-programme buildout as a GC blueprint) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F071_AWS_AI_for_Security_and_Security_for_AI_2025]] — AWS's explicit sequencing (AI inventory including "shadow generative AI" detection → AI-specific risk assessment → organisation-wide policy → documentation → defined roles/shared-responsibility clarity → continuous monitoring, with feedback loops) is a concrete governance-capacity-building sequence usable as a maturity-progression checklist, distinct from DASF's (F070) control catalogue and closer in structure to ISACA's (F038) staged maturity model.
- (Batch 18 — Cross-disciplinary risk function as organisational-capacity architecture) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F072_WEF_Oxford_AI_Cybersecurity_Balancing_Risks_Rewards_2025]] — WEF/Oxford's explicit call for a cross-disciplinary AI risk function (legal, cyber, compliance, technology, risk, HR, ethics, front-line business units) is a governance-capacity-building specification at the organisational-design level, derived from multi-stakeholder workshops rather than a single vendor's internal practice — useful as board/CRO-facing capacity-building collateral distinct from the more technical capacity instruments (F070, F071) elsewhere in this batch.
- (Indexing gap closed 2026-07-15 — originally missed at node creation, 2026-06-19) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F045_PwC_TPRM_Digital_Geopolitical_Era_2025]] — PwC Netherlands TPRM white paper's AI-enabled TPRM tooling (LeAh, CREANCE.AI, DMS AI Platform, Quickscan) represents governance-capacity augmentation at the third-party/vendor-risk layer specifically; the paper's "Good TPRM → Great TPRM" progression is itself a capacity-maturity model structurally parallel to the CMMI-style scales used elsewhere in this corpus (cf. F038 ISACA). The "Five Board Questions for TPRM Oversight" framework is a board-level capacity diagnostic comparable to MindForge's C15 Talent/Capability consideration (F054).
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-16 — Management-theory grounding for oversight distinctiveness) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F098_Recker_Berente_This_Is_Research_Podcast_Episodes11to25_2021]] — the podcast's Episode 17 ("Strategic Directions for AI") claim that "executive oversight must be qualitatively different from the oversight applied to passive IT systems" because AI systems make decisions autonomously is an independent, management-theory-derived articulation of enterprise-level governance-capacity requirements for autonomous systems, corroborating from a different disciplinary angle the runtime-governance argument already developed in [[Agentic_AI_Governance]]'s Five Structural Breaks. Theoretical/argumentative source, not an empirical capacity-maturity instrument — cite as academic grounding, not as evidence of capacity levels achieved anywhere in the corpus.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-20 — Sovereign repair capacity as critical infrastructure governance deficit) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F129_Pandey_Kumar_Shore_to_Core_Subsea_Networks_2025]] — India's absence of a sovereign subsea cable repair vessel, lack of a designated nodal authority for cable repair coordination, and the requirement for a DoT official's physical presence on repair vessels are named governance-capacity deficits for critical infrastructure. The recommended six-point resilience framework (National Subsea Cable Policy → CII classification → sovereign repair vessel → Marine Spatial Planning → QUAD engagement) is itself a governance-capacity-building roadmap for a domain where India currently operates at Maturity Level 1–2 (ad hoc, reliant on private/foreign consortia, no national policy). Useful as a cross-sector instance of Governance_Capacity extending beyond the AI/BFSI domain to physical infrastructure governance.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-20 — Digital inequality as governance-capacity deficit at population level) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F130_UNESCO_AI_Future_Education_2025|F130]] — UNESCO anthology's digital inequality figures (90% of adolescent girls in low-income countries remain offline as of 2025 ⚠#301 HIGH; women 35% of STEM students globally; 26% of AI/data workforce) articulate a Governance_Capacity deficit at the *population and human-capital level* rather than the institutional or infrastructure level: a state cannot govern AI effectively, or claim high Stakeholder Inclusion scores in fsQCA calibration, if its population cannot access AI systems or participate in producing them. Extends the corpus's Governance_Capacity instances from infrastructure-level deficits (F129's subsea cable repair capacity; F044's 89% lacking ML security tools) to the human-capital dimension of governance capacity. Primarily relevant to fsQCA SI (Stakeholder Inclusion) condition calibration — jurisdictions with high population offline rates structurally constrain inclusive governance regardless of formal consultation architecture.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-16 — Capability-boundary-location as a distinct governance-capacity competency) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F101_Recker_Berente_This_Is_Research_Podcast_Episodes61to90_2023_2025]] — the podcast's treatment of the Jagged Technological Frontier (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023) frames AI competence as non-linear and contextually variable rather than a smooth capability gradient; an organisation's ability to locate this frontier for its own use cases — rather than assume uniform AI capability or incompetence — is itself a governance-capacity competency, complementing F098's oversight-structure instance with a capability-assessment dimension of the same construct. Theoretical/argumentative source, not an empirical capacity-maturity instrument.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-08-09 — Institution-before-regulation, the corpus's only end-to-end datable instance) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/CEA/CEA_3_Cyber_Security_Power_Sector_Regulations_2026]] — CSIRT-Power was administratively inaugurated **September 2024** and formally constituted in binding text by CEA_3 Chapter II on **31 July 2026**, with fourteen enumerated functions, evidence-collection powers over any entity, and authority over vendors as well as entities. **The sequencing gap measures ≈22 months.** Of the corpus's four institution-before-regulation instances (CSIRT-Power, the AA regime/DEPA, Sahamati, BIS/IS-ISO-IEC-42001 adoption), this is the only one now datable from both ends, which makes it the reference case for the pattern. It is also the direct inverse of [[07_Institutions/Corpus/DPB/DPB_1_Establishment_and_Vacancy_2025_2026]] — formal authority with no capacity — and the two together bracket the sequencing pathology: capacity-before-authority resolves in ~22 months; authority-before-capacity has not resolved at all. Separately, CEA_3 builds capacity architecture at three tiers (CSIRT-Power, Authority-designated **sub-sectoral** CSIRTs per reg 4(4), and entity-level 24×7 Information Security Divisions per reg 5(9)) — the most layered sectoral cyber-capacity design in the Indian corpus. ⚠ Note that reg 5(9) is one of six regulations whose commencement is **deferred to separate orders**, so the entity-tier capacity requirement is not in force on 1 April 2027.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-08-09 — Principles-and-projects substituting for a named audit institution) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/Parliament/Parliament_2_Unstarred_Question_2988_Institutional_Framework_Auditing_AI_2026]] — a direct Lok Sabha question asking whether an institutional framework exists to *audit* AI algorithms in government/citizen-facing services is answered with the Sutras (principles), 13 named Safe & Trusted AI research projects, and the NABCB/ISO 42001 voluntary certification track — no dedicated statutory audit body for citizen-facing state AI is named. A clean instance of stated governance capacity (principles, committees, international standing) substituting for demonstrated governance capacity (a named audit institution) when Parliament asks the pointed institutional-design question directly. Also carries an unresolved contradiction with this vault's own NABCB-accreditation-gap finding — see [[06_Frameworks/ISO_IEC_42001]].
