# **Legitimacy**

**Type:** Standard Concept  
**Status:** Active  
**Last Updated:** 2026-06-27 (Batch 15 instances added)  
**Theoretical Lineage:** Suchman (1995); institutional theory; regulatory governance

---

## **Definition**

Legitimacy in this research programme is treated as a property of AI governance frameworks and regulatory architectures rather than of individual AI systems, organisations, or decisions. Drawing on Suchman’s distinction between pragmatic, cognitive, and moral legitimacy, the construct captures the degree to which governance arrangements are perceived as appropriate, desirable, and institutionally acceptable within a given jurisdictional context. Within the fsQCA research design, legitimacy functions as the primary outcome condition to be explained: the central analytical question is which configurations of governance conditions generate legitimate AI governance outcomes across jurisdictions.

## **Application in This Research**

Within [[fsQCA_Thesis_Chapter]], legitimacy serves as the outcome variable against which combinations of Enforcement Architecture (EA), Systemic Regulatory conditions (SR), Regulatory Logic (RL), and Stakeholder Inclusion (SI) are evaluated for sufficiency. Within [[P1_SLR_RG]], legitimacy functions as the contribution anchor through which governance typologies are assessed and validated. Across the SLR corpus, legitimacy is consistently articulated as a governance objective by AI governance frameworks, providing the conceptual foundation for comparative analysis. The construct is theoretically linked to [[Institutional_Coherence]], which is hypothesised to contribute positively to legitimate governance outcomes by reducing structural ambiguity and coordination failures.

## **Operationalisation in fsQCA**

Legitimacy is calibrated using external implementation evidence rather than the same ADICO-derived indicators used to measure the causal conditions. This separation is a deliberate methodological choice designed to avoid circularity between explanatory variables and outcome measures. Calibration draws upon implementation trajectories, post-2024 amendments, evidence of institutional adoption, enforcement activity, and broader governance acceptance signals. Calibration anchors are defined as 0.00 for no external legitimacy indicators, 0.33 for partial adoption signals, 0.67 for demonstrated implementation evidence, and 1.00 for multiple strong legitimacy indicators including international adoption, demonstrated enforcement activity, or sustained post-hoc institutional validation.

## **Boundary Conditions**

- Legitimacy is distinct from compliance; compliance reflects behavioural conformity with rules, whereas legitimacy reflects institutional and societal acceptance of governance arrangements.
- Legitimacy is distinct from effectiveness; a governance framework may be widely accepted yet ineffective in practice, or effective without broad institutional acceptance.
- Legitimacy functions exclusively as the outcome condition within the fsQCA model and is not treated as a causal condition in the configurational analysis.

## **Theoretical Positioning**

Legitimacy is theoretically anchored in Suchman's (1995) tripartite framework distinguishing pragmatic legitimacy (stakeholder utility), cognitive legitimacy (taken-for-granted acceptance), and moral legitimacy (normative appropriateness). Within regulatory governance scholarship, legitimacy is treated as a relational property of governance arrangements rather than an intrinsic characteristic of rules or institutions — it must be earned through demonstrated performance, procedural fairness, and institutional acceptance. Black (2008) extends this by distinguishing input legitimacy (participation and process) from output legitimacy (effectiveness and outcomes), a distinction with direct relevance to how the fsQCA model calibrates legitimacy as an outcome condition. Within information systems research, legitimacy connects to technology acceptance and institutional adoption frameworks that examine how governance arrangements become embedded in organisational practice. The deliberate separation of legitimacy from compliance and effectiveness in this research — treating a framework as legitimate when it is institutionally accepted, not merely when it is followed or when it produces good outcomes — reflects Suchman's foundational distinction and disciplines the fsQCA calibration accordingly.

## **Future Reuse Opportunities**

- Use the fsQCA legitimacy outcome calibration as the empirical foundation for a P1 typology of AI governance frameworks, classifying frameworks by the configuration of conditions that generates legitimate governance outcomes across jurisdictions.
- Develop executive education content contrasting legitimacy with compliance and effectiveness, using Indian BFSI governance as a case where instruments may be followed without being institutionally accepted as appropriate governance arrangements.
- Extend the legitimacy construct into the Regulatory Complexity Handbook as a normative benchmark, arguing that governance architectures characterised by high Regulatory Parallelism generate lower output legitimacy because enterprises cannot discern coherent regulatory intent.
- Use India's RAM submission to UNESCO and the IndiaAI principles adoption trajectory as empirical material for thought-leadership content examining how principles-based governance frameworks acquire or fail to acquire legitimacy over time.

## **Instances in Corpus**

- (Batch 12 — Strongest global normative legitimacy signal / 193-state moral legitimacy) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_18_UNESCO_Recommendation_Ethics_AI_2021]] — UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021): adopted by all 193 UNESCO member states at the 41st General Conference (November 25, 2021). Suchman (1995) **moral legitimacy**: the broadest normative endorsement of any AI ethics instrument in the corpus. For fsQCA calibration: UNESCO adoption is the closest available indicator of 1.00 on the moral-legitimacy dimension, but does not translate to 1.00 on the empirical legitimacy outcome without implementation evidence. India's RAM submission (⚠VERIFY status) would be the implementation-legitimacy signal; absence of an operative instrument means India sits at 0.33–0.67 on the fsQCA legitimacy scale despite having formally adopted the most widely ratified global AI ethics framework.
- (Batch 12 — G20 multilateral legitimacy / Global South governance posture) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_17_G20_South_Africa_Leaders_Declaration_2025]] — G20 South Africa Leaders Declaration 2025 (AI chapters): multilateral endorsement from 20 major economies — including India, US, China, EU, Japan, and African Union — of development-oriented AI governance framing. Suchman **pragmatic legitimacy**: G20 SA signals that the "development orientation + voluntary governance" posture is acceptable to the Global South majority of G20, not merely a developing-country exception. This legitimacy signal constrains the feasibility of India adopting mandatory EU-style AI governance: a G20 consensus exists that would need to be overridden for India to shift toward EA-heavy architecture. For fsQCA SI (Stakeholder Inclusion) calibration: G20 Leaders Declaration is the highest-level democratic accountability signal for a member state's AI governance posture.
- (Batch 12 — Governance architecture type as legitimacy-generation mechanism) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F032_Maas_Architectures_Global_AI_Governance_2025]] — Maas, *Architectures of Global AI Governance* (OUP 2025): regime complex theory frames governance architecture type as a legitimacy-generation mechanism. Hierarchical architectures (EU AI Act) generate output legitimacy through enforcement and demonstrable compliance. Polycentric architectures (India / Japan / Australia "principles" cluster) generate input legitimacy through inclusion and deliberation but weaker output legitimacy (no demonstrable compliance outcome). Maas provides the academic grounding for why India's governance posture generates pragmatic and cognitive legitimacy (industry acceptance, no enforcement cost) but sits at the lower end of output legitimacy calibration in the fsQCA model.
- (Batch 12 — "Bridge powers" / geopolitical legitimacy positioning) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F033_Oxford_Blueprint_Multinational_AI_2025]] — Oxford Martin AIGI Blueprint for Multinational Advanced AI Development (2025): "bridge powers" framing positions India's governance regime (DPDP + MeitY + RBI FREE-AI) as a structural legitimacy asset in geopolitical AI governance — India can credibly position itself as a reliable AI partner for both Global South and Global North precisely because its governance architecture provides demonstrable (if not fully operationalised) privacy, ethics, and risk-management commitments. Compute concentration data (≈5% global, ⚠V-156) contextualises the legitimacy ceiling: India's governance claim is credible to a point constrained by structural dependence on non-Indian compute infrastructure.
- (2026-07-15 — Gap-fill: third-party certification as output legitimacy) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_22_EU_AI_Act_Conformity_Assessment_Notified_Bodies]] — the EU AI Act's notified-body/CE-marking regime is the corpus's clearest instance of Suchman **output legitimacy** generated through independent third-party verification rather than self-attestation or regulator reporting. Notified-body designation infrastructure has been live since Aug 2025 with near-zero bodies designated by mid-2026, and the underlying compliance deadline itself was deferred (Digital Omnibus, to 2 Dec 2027) — a case where formal legitimacy-generating architecture exists in law well ahead of operational legitimacy in practice, sharpening the Maas (F032) hierarchical-architecture output-legitimacy argument with an implementation-lag qualifier.
- (Batch 13 — International human rights law as legitimacy floor / Türk claim) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F039_ICAAD_KWM_AI_Harm_Human_Rights]] — ICAAD + KWM rights-harm mapping: Türk (UN High Commissioner for Human Rights) quote — "AI that cannot comply with international human rights law must be banned or suspended" — frames international human rights law as a *legitimacy floor* for AI systems, not merely an aspirational standard. **Output legitimacy** in Suchman's sense: AI systems that produce rights-violating outputs lack legitimacy regardless of the governance process that produced them. EU AI Act Art. 27 FRIA obligation (from 2 August 2026) operationalizes this as legal mechanism. For fsQCA LG calibration: jurisdictions with FRIA-equivalent obligations score higher on output legitimacy than those without.
- (Batch 13 — Procedural legitimacy / access to justice and reasoned decisions) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F040_ELI_Guiding_Principles_ADM_EU]] — ELI 12 Guiding Principles for ADM in the EU: Principle 8 (No Limitations to Rights and Access to Justice) + Principle 10 (Human Review of Significant Decisions) + Principle 11 (Responsible ADM) constitute the **procedural legitimacy** architecture for ADM governance — systems that deny contestability, human review, or dignity protections fail on procedural legitimacy grounds. UNCITRAL Model Law Principle 2 (Non-discrimination against ADM) provides the converse legitimacy claim: ADM systems should receive *equal* legal recognition to human decision-makers when they meet the same substantive standards. For India: DPDP Act 2023 provides limited contestability for data processing; no ADM-specific procedural legitimacy obligations comparable to ELI Principles 6, 8, and 10 exist.
- (Batch 14 — AI law reform legitimacy / India has no equivalent statutory process) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F046_UK_LawCommission_AI_and_Law_DiscussionPaper_2025]] — UK Law Commission AI and the Law Discussion Paper (2025): statutory law reform consultation backed by parliamentary authority — the institutional form generating output legitimacy for legal system adaptation to AI. India has no equivalent statutory law reform body process for AI, producing an output legitimacy gap in AI liability doctrine: the Indian legal system has not formally mapped where existing law fails to accommodate AI's seven distinctive properties. In Suchman's terms, output legitimacy requires demonstrated governance performance — a legal system that cannot attribute liability for AI-caused harms lacks output legitimacy at the legal doctrine layer. Input legitimacy gap also: the UK Law Commission consultation invites substantive stakeholder input from lawyers, technologists, and civil society; India's absence of an equivalent process is an input legitimacy deficit as well.
- (Batch 14 — Decoupling as legitimacy failure mechanism / deployer liability as pragmatic legitimacy) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F047_BAIR_Berkeley_Responsible_GenAI_Playbook_2025]] — Smith et al. (BAIR/Berkeley Haas, 2025): Institutional Theory "decoupling" — formal responsible AI commitments (OL-1 executive statements, published RAI policies) failing to translate to operational practice (PM-level micro-moments) — is the governance legitimacy failure mechanism in responsible AI. Input legitimacy (deliberative commitment, stakeholder engagement at the leadership level) is decoupled from output legitimacy (actual responsible governance outcomes in deployment). *Moffatt v. Air Canada* (BC Civil Resolution Tribunal, 2024) and *Mata v. Avianca* (SDNY, 2023) demonstrate **pragmatic legitimacy** in action: courts assign deployer accountability for AI outputs regardless of the deployer's formal governance policy, on the grounds that deployers cannot legitimately disclaim liability for systems they chose to deploy. The decoupling mechanism (institutional theory) explains why pragmatic legitimacy enforcement (case law) is structurally necessary.
- (Batch 14 — "Foundational hypocrisy" as legitimacy void / binding vs. voluntary as legitimacy-generation variable) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F048_FLI_AI_Safety_Index_Winter_2025]] — Future of Life Institute AI Safety Index Winter 2025 (December 2025): "foundational hypocrisy" — the systematic gap between frontier AI companies' public safety commitments and their operational safety infrastructure — is the corpus's sharpest governance legitimacy failure designation. In Suchman's terms: cognitive legitimacy (companies are widely recognised as AI leaders with safety commitments) is decoupled from output legitimacy (all companies score D/F on Existential Safety for the second consecutive edition). EU AI Code of Practice failure by all 8 companies demonstrates that even the most binding governance legitimacy standard currently applicable has not generated output legitimacy outcomes. Chinese companies' paradox (higher baseline accountability indicators from binding domestic regulations — GB45438-2025, Deep Synthesis Regulations, Interim Measures — despite D/D- overall grades) illustrates the legitimacy-generation mechanism: binding obligations generate regulatory legitimacy even at low overall governance scores, while voluntary Western commitments do not. For fsQCA LG calibration: binding vs. voluntary obligation is the structural variable for legitimacy-generation across jurisdictions.
- (Batch 15 — Pragmatic and moral legitimacy of RAI governance) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Papagiannidis_Mikalef_Conboy_RAI_Governance_Framework_2025]] (F050) — legitimacy as outcome: the paper's framework explicitly models legitimacy and stakeholder trust as positive outcomes of mature relational and structural RAI governance practices. This provides an information-systems-grounded confirmation of Suchman's legitimacy-generation logic, validating the fsQCA causal model.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Fundamental-rights grounding as legitimacy mechanism) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/FRA_EU_Assessing_High_Risk_AI_Fundamental_Rights]] (F053) — FRA FRIA guide: fundamental rights grounding of FRIA is a legitimacy-conferring mechanism in Suchman's **moral legitimacy** sense. A governance system predicated on rights (EU Charter) is more morally legitimate than purely utilitarian risk-management frameworks. For fsQCA LG calibration: EU rights-grounded regime scores higher on moral legitimacy than jurisdictions with ethics-principles-only frameworks (Australia, India). Rights-based FRIA is thus both an output-legitimacy instrument (enforcement) and moral-legitimacy instrument (grounding).
- (Batch 15 continuation — Industry consortium co-production as legitimacy architecture) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/MindForge_MAS_AI_Risk_Management_Executive_Handbook]] (F054) — MindForge's 24-FI consortium under MAS facilitation is a **pragmatic legitimacy** architecture (Suchman): industry co-production generates norm-setting legitimacy that neither pure regulator-issued guidance nor pure industry association output could achieve alone. Cognitive legitimacy through participation of major FIs (AIA, Citi, DBS, HSBC, StanChart, UBS, UOB, etc.) — the framework is legitimate because who authored it is legitimate. India has no equivalent BFSI-industry consortium AI governance instrument — RBI FREE-AI Committee is regulator-convened without industry co-production authority.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Proactive government disclosure as legitimacy mechanism) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_20_Australia_DTA_AI_Impact_Assessment_Tool]] — DTA AIIA Tool: proactive public disclosure of High risk AI Impact Assessments is a **procedural legitimacy** mechanism enabling external civil-society scrutiny of government AI decisions. Transparency as legitimacy — the government's willingness to publicly disclose High-risk AI usage decisions is itself a legitimacy-generating act. Combined with SES-Band-2 elevated approval chain, AIIA architecture generates procedural + institutional legitimacy. India government-as-deployer AI has no equivalent proactive disclosure legitimacy mechanism.
- (Batch 18 — Multi-stakeholder derivation as a legitimacy-conferring process) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F072_WEF_Oxford_AI_Cybersecurity_Balancing_Risks_Rewards_2025]] — WEF AI Governance Alliance + Oxford GCSCC's cybersecurity guidance is explicitly derived from structured workshops/interviews across business, government, academia, and civil society, rather than a single company's internal practice. In Suchman's terms, this is a **pragmatic legitimacy** instance parallel to F054 (MindForge's 24-FI consortium) but at the cross-industry rather than BFSI-sector-specific level — corroborating that consensus-derivation method, not just institutional seniority, is a distinct legitimacy-generating variable in the corpus.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-20 — Unilateral vendor action as legitimacy violation / participatory co-design as legitimacy generator) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F130_UNESCO_AI_Future_Education_2025|F130]] — Two contrasting legitimacy instances from the same chapter (Gulson & Sellar, p. 117): (a) **Legitimacy violation** — Google embedding 30 AI tools in Google Classroom without explanation or consent (June 2025) is a case of platform-power unilateral action that bypasses stakeholder consultation entirely, removing the process legitimacy basis for any governance arrangement that follows. In Suchman's terms, no input legitimacy (participation), no procedural legitimacy (deliberation), and therefore no basis for claiming cognitive or moral legitimacy for the resulting governance arrangement. (b) **Pragmatic legitimacy** — Brazil's co-creation of *Guia IA para Educadores* with 60 educators, CIEB, and the Ministry of Education (UNESCO/CIEB, 2024) demonstrates participatory legitimacy-generation through deliberate co-design, parallel to MindForge's 24-FI BFSI consortium (F054). These two instances are the sharpest contrast in the corpus between legitimacy-through-exclusion-failure and legitimacy-through-co-production within the same sector and governance domain. Useful for the fsQCA SI (Stakeholder Inclusion) condition calibration — unilateral vendor deployment without consultation is a scored SI-absence instance, while documented co-design with sectoral practitioners is a scored SI-presence instance.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-20 — Consultation as mandatory legitimacy condition for Cable Protection Zone governance) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F129_Pandey_Kumar_Shore_to_Core_Subsea_Networks_2025|F129]] — the report's explicit position that Cable Protection Zones (CPZs) "must not be mandatory in nature and that they be declared through consultations with wide-ranging stakeholders," combined with the recommendation for "continuous dialogue between fishermen, cable authorities, and government instrumentalities," frames consultation as a legitimacy-generating prerequisite for physical infrastructure governance. In Suchman's terms, this is a **pragmatic legitimacy** instance: CPZ restrictions imposed without fishing community buy-in would face non-compliance and social resistance, removing their practical effectiveness. The Oregon Fishermen's Cable Committee (US) is cited as a comparator model. Non-AI-governance instance; notable because the report treats consultation not merely as procedural good practice but as structurally necessary for the governance arrangement to function — legitimacy as operationally enabling, not merely normatively desirable. Extends the corpus's legitimacy-through-consultation instances (F054 MindForge consortium; F072 WEF/Oxford multi-stakeholder derivation) into the physical critical-infrastructure domain.
