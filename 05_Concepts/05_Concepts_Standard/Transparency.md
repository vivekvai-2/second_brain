# **Transparency**

**Type:** Standard Concept  
**Status:** Active  
**Last Updated:** 2026-06-27 (Batch 15 instances added)  
**Theoretical Lineage:** IS governance; regulatory governance; AI governance frameworks

---

## **Definition**

Transparency in this research programme is defined as the obligation to make the decision logic, data sources, performance characteristics, governance controls, and accountability structures of AI systems accessible to relevant stakeholders in a manner appropriate to their role and the system’s risk level. The concept is distinct from explainability because transparency concerns the visibility and availability of governance information, whereas explainability concerns the interpretability of model outputs and internal logic. It is also distinct from disclosure, which is a discrete act of communication, while transparency is a continuing governance obligation that varies according to stakeholder needs, regulatory requirements, and system risk profiles.

## **Application in This Research**

Within [[P3_BFSI_JEIM]], transparency obligations appear as coded regulatory requirements relating to audit trails, model governance documentation, and supervisory visibility mandated by RBI and SEBI instruments. Within [[P4_Doctrinal_IJLIT]], transparency functions as a normative requirement of the redesigned Three Lines of Defence architecture, with particular emphasis on regulatory auditability of AI-supported decisions and trading systems. Within [[fsQCA_Thesis_Chapter]], transparency is incorporated into the Regulatory Logic (RL) condition, reflecting the expectation that higher-risk AI systems should be subject to stronger transparency obligations than lower-risk systems. Within the [[AI_Governance]] domain and associated governance frameworks, transparency appears as a core governance principle, reflected in the IndiaAI design principles, the transparency obligations of the EU AI Act, and the governance vocabulary of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

## **Boundary Conditions**

- Transparency is not equivalent to explainability; an AI system may provide comprehensive governance documentation and audit evidence while remaining technically difficult to interpret.
- Transparency obligations are risk-proportionate, with higher-risk systems facing stronger requirements for documentation, auditability, reporting, and oversight than lower-risk systems.
- Transparency directed toward regulators through audit trails and compliance evidence serves a different governance function from transparency directed toward users through disclosures and notices, although both forms may be required simultaneously.

## **Theoretical Positioning**

Transparency draws its theoretical grounding from three distinct but complementary traditions. Within information systems research, transparency connects to concepts of information visibility, algorithmic accountability, and explainability in sociotechnical systems, with Wieringa (2020) and subsequent work on algorithmic auditing providing the most relevant IS-specific framing. Regulatory governance theory treats transparency as a governance instrument rather than a value — Fung, Graham, and Weil's (2007) targeted transparency framework demonstrates that disclosure obligations are effective only when information reaches audiences capable of acting on it, a condition that shapes how transparency obligations are designed in RBI and SEBI instruments. Within AI governance scholarship, the distinction between transparency directed at regulators through audit trails and transparency directed at users through notices and disclosures has become a structuring debate, with the EU AI Act operationalising different transparency obligations for different stakeholder categories. Within PRIS, transparency is treated as a governance observable — its presence or absence in coded regulatory instruments is detectable through ADICO analysis, and its risk-proportionality is a calibrated property of the Regulatory Logic (RL) condition in the fsQCA model.

## **Future Reuse Opportunities**

- Use the distinction between regulator-facing transparency (audit trails, governance documentation) and user-facing transparency (notices, disclosures) as a structuring framework for the BFSI AI Governance Handbook chapter on audit and board oversight.
- Develop LinkedIn thought-leadership content examining the difference between transparency as a compliance artefact and transparency as a genuine governance mechanism, using RBI FREE-AI's audit trail requirements and SEBI's quarterly reporting obligations as concrete examples.
- Extend the transparency analysis from P3 and P4 into a comparative study of how different regulatory instruments operationalise transparency obligations — distinguishing disclosure-first architectures (SEBI 2019 circular) from risk-management architectures (NIST AI RMF Govern function).
- Incorporate the risk-proportionality of transparency obligations as a calibration criterion within the fsQCA Regulatory Logic (RL) condition, testing whether higher transparency obligation specificity correlates with legitimate governance outcomes across the ten-case corpus.

## **Instances in Corpus**

- (Batch 7) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/SCI_1_White_Paper_AI_and_Judiciary_2025]] — Chapter 6 disclosure requirements (citation of AI tool/version/prompt + audit trail) for judicial AI use, plus a "red flag indicators of AI-generated work" checklist, are user/regulator-facing transparency mechanisms applied to a judicial rather than BFSI/executive context.
- (Batch 7) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/IRDAI/IRDAI_1_Maintenance_and_Sharing_of_Information_Regulations_2025]] — public-disclosure provisions for non-confidential insurer data (financial health, claim-settlement ratios, customer-satisfaction metrics) extend regulator-facing transparency into a public-facing dimension, pending primary-source verification.
- (Batch 7) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/CERT_In/CERT_In_1_Comprehensive_Cyber_Security_Audit_Policy_Guidelines_2025]] — CVSS/EPSS/CWE/CVE mandatory mapping standardises audit-finding disclosure format across all audited entities, a structured-transparency mechanism distinct from narrative disclosure obligations elsewhere in the corpus.
- (Batch 10 — Consumer right to explanation) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/International_12_HCJP_Legal_AI_BFSI_2025]] — HCJP France (June 2025): documents the **right to explanation of AI decisions in financial services** as embedded in both EU AI Act and pre-existing MiFID II/CRD frameworks — explainability as a statutory consumer right, not merely a risk-management governance practice. This is the corpus's clearest statement of user-facing transparency as a **legally enforceable consumer obligation** rather than a supervisory tool. India gap: RBI FREE-AI and SEBI instruments frame transparency/explainability as supervisory and governance tools; no statutory consumer right to AI explanation exists in any Indian BFSI instrument as of Batch 10 corpus. An analytically significant EU/India transparency-framing divergence for P3 and P4.
- (2026-07-15) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F085_Sudjianto_Zhang_Model_Validation_Practice_Banking_2024]] — the corpus's most granular explainability-*methods* taxonomy (SHAP/LIME/PDP/ALE, interpretable architectures, fANOVA decomposition), directly supplying the technical methods that satisfy the CFPB Circular 2022-03 post-hoc-explanation-accuracy requirement and complementing the Model Cards/Datasheets documentation-*standard* pair (F080/F081) with the actual validation techniques those standards would report.
- (Batch 10 — Transparency recommendation without enactment) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/TRAI/TRAI_2]] — TRAI AI and Big Data Recommendations (July 2023): Recommendation R2 calls for "transparency in AI systems" as one of seven national AI governance recommendations submitted to government. Not yet enacted as a binding direction (⚠V-TRAI5: advisory body established?; ⚠V-TRAI6: DoT acceptance?). A *recommendation-without-enactment* transparency instance — TRAI has named transparency as a governance priority at the national level; this recommendation has not produced a binding transparency obligation in any Indian regulatory instrument as of TRAI_1 (Feb 2026). Adds to the Governance_Debt evidence base: strategic transparency commitment without instrument-level operationalisation.
- (Batch 10 — Systemic risk transparency) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_10]] — BoE FS in Focus (Apr 2025): FPC names "greater transparency about how AI is used and its impacts" as one of its risk-mitigation actions for financial institutions. **Regulator-facing systemic transparency** — distinct from both entity-level disclosure (SEBI quarterly reports) and consumer-facing transparency (HCJP/EU AI Act): this is transparency directed at macro-financial-stability assessment, requiring financial institutions to disclose AI use in aggregate terms for systemic risk analysis. No Indian equivalent: RBI FREE-AI and SEBI instruments address entity-level and supervisory-facing transparency but not systemic-level AI-use disclosure for financial stability monitoring.
- (Batch 11 — Privacy policy as regulator-mandated user transparency) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_7]] — RBI (Digital Lending) Directions, 2025: RU5 mandates a comprehensive privacy policy explaining AI use of customer data in credit decisions — **regulator-mandated, user-facing** transparency for AI-assisted lending. This is the corpus's strongest India-side user-facing transparency obligation to date; however, it does not reach the EU AI Act standard of a statutory consumer *right* to AI explanation (HCJP/International_12 finding) — a privacy policy disclosure versus a legally actionable explanation right. Clarifies where Indian BFSI user-facing transparency sits relative to the EU benchmark.
- (Batch 11 — Earliest SEBI regulator-facing transparency mechanism) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/SEBI/SEBI_17]] — SEBI Reporting for AI/ML by Market Intermediaries (January 4, 2019): RU1/RU4/RU5 (quarterly reporting + control safeguards documentation + abnormal behaviour disclosure) constitute the corpus's **earliest SEBI structured AI transparency obligation** — directed at regulators (via Stock Exchanges/Depositories → SEBI), not at investors. This establishes a critical typological distinction: SEBI's 2019 transparency architecture is regulator-facing inventory transparency; user/investor-facing transparency was not addressed until SEBI_14 (2025). Six years of regulator-facing transparency before any investor-facing transparency obligation — maps the transparency-dimension of SEBI's governance arc.
- (Batch 12 — Most widely ratified transparency principle globally / principle-without-instrument gap) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_18_UNESCO_Recommendation_Ethics_AI_2021]] — UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021): Article 42 (Transparency and Explainability) — "AI actors should be transparent about their AI systems and their use of AI" — adopted by 193 UNESCO member states; the corpus's most broadly accepted transparency principle. Zero enforcement mechanism. Critical distinction: UNESCO's transparency principle covers the full governance arc (development / deployment / use), whereas Indian BFSI instruments address narrower, instrument-specific obligations (SEBI_17 regulator-facing / RBI_7 RU5 user-facing / HCJP statutory consumer right). UNESCO provides the benchmark against which the India-EU transparency gap can be framed as a principle-to-instrument operationalisation failure.
- (Batch 12 — IOSCO 8 principles including Transparency / fourth transparency type: market-integrity level) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_19_IOSCO_AI_Capital_Markets_CR_2025]] — IOSCO AI in Capital Markets CR/01/2025 (IOSCOPD788, source confirmed 2026-06-19): Transparency is IOSCO's first governance principle (of eight) for AI in capital markets. IOSCO's framing operates at the systemic market-integrity level — intermediaries' AI use must be transparent to regulators for market integrity monitoring, not merely to customers. This is a **market-integrity transparency** dimension distinct from: regulator-facing inventory transparency (SEBI_17 quarterly reporting, 2019), user-facing disclosure transparency (RBI_7 RU5 privacy policy, 2025), statutory consumer right to explanation (HCJP/EU AI Act, Batch 10), and systemic financial-stability transparency (BoE FPC, Batch 10). IOSCO adds a fourth transparency type to the corpus's transparency typology.
- (Batch 12 — Technical transparency verification / legal-to-audit bridge, India gap) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F034_Buscemi_Assessing_High_Risk_AI_2025]] — Buscemi et al. (arXiv, 2025): the 11 verification macro-categories include "Transparency" mapping EU AI Act Article 13 obligations (transparency to users; transparency to deployers, Article 25) to ISO/IEC 42001 audit controls. A testable transparency verification methodology. India gap: RBI FREE-AI and SEBI instruments establish transparency obligations (audit trails, governance documentation) but India has no equivalent legal-to-technical transparency verification standard bridging obligation text to auditable control evidence. F034's transparency verification methodology is directly applicable as a technical standards gap for India BFSI AI governance.
- (Batch 13 — DLP + sensitivity labels as transparency enforcement mechanisms) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F035_Microsoft_Administering_Governing_Agents]] — Microsoft Purview's sensitivity labelling and DLP policies operationalize transparency at the data-access control layer: which agent can access what labelled data, logged and auditable. A vendor-layer implementation of transparency infrastructure complementary to regulatory obligations (RBI_7 RU5, SEBI_17 quarterly reporting).
- (2026-07-16 — Plain-language explainability-tradeoff framing) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F115_Verma_Sentiment_Analysis_Smart_Society_2022|F115]] — Verma (NITIE Mumbai) cites Calegari, Ciatto & Omicini's (2020) sub-symbolic AI ("opaque data-based system," i.e. deep learning) vs. symbolic AI ("inspectability, interpretability, explainability") distinction — a concise, two-category framing complementary to F085's granular SHAP/LIME/PDP/ALE methods taxonomy, useful for practitioner-facing (Board/CRO) explanations of the transparency/performance trade-off.
- (Batch 13 — Agent identification and data flow tracking) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F036_NIST_NCCoE_Agent_Identity_Authorization]] — Focus Areas 1 (Identification), 5 (Logging/Audit), and 6 (Data Flow Tracking) constitute the technical transparency architecture for agent systems; the document distinguishes traceability (logged records exist) from transparency (human-intelligible explanation) — a distinction with direct implications for AI Act explainability obligations.
- (Batch 13 — Evaluation transparency standard / editorial control + anti-redaction) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F041_AEF1_Minimum_Operating_Conditions_AI_Evaluations]] — AEF-1 MOC 4 (Transparent Methods and Results) is the most detailed operational definition of evaluation transparency in the corpus: non-contingent release, no provider redaction authority over concerning findings, methodological transparency, redaction disclaimer when any redaction occurs. Adds a fifth transparency type: **evaluation-process transparency** (distinct from market-integrity / regulator-facing inventory / user-facing disclosure / systemic-stability transparency documented in prior batches).
- (Batch 13 — Traceability ≠ transparency / ADM reasoned decisions) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F040_ELI_Guiding_Principles_ADM_EU]] — ELI Principle 5 (Traceable Decisions) + Principle 6 (Reasoned Decisions) make the analytically important distinction: traceability (logging by design; auditable record) is necessary but not sufficient for transparency (human-intelligible explanation / reasons accessible to affected person). Complexity or opacity of ADM is not a valid ground for unreasoned or arbitrary decisions. This distinction directly maps onto AI Act explainability gap analysis in India.
- (Batch 14 — Shadow AI as transparency deficit; breach disclosure as transparency obligation) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F042_IBM_Cost_Data_Breach_Report_2025]] — Shadow AI (ungoverned AI deployment, USD 670K breach premium) is a transparency failure: organisations cannot govern what they cannot see; 63% lacking AI governance policies implies AI operations are opaque to boards and regulators. Correlate: DPDP Act s.8(6) breach notification = a transparency obligation triggered by the AI security failures IBM documents.
- (Batch 14 — Model-level transparency gap in mitigation taxonomy) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F043_MIT_AI_Risk_Mitigation_Taxonomy_2025]] — Transparency & Accountability category (~21% of mitigations) covers disclosure and audit requirements at the output/process level; model alignment gap (<1%) reveals that transparency at the model-internals level is absent from governance frameworks globally — no instrument requires explanation of how model goals are formed.
- (Batch 14 — Transparency obligations under EU Digital Decade framework) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F045_PwC_TPRM_Digital_Geopolitical_Era_2025]] — DORA (concentration risk transparency to regulators), AI Act Art.13 (transparency to users of high-risk AI systems), and NIS2 (incident transparency) together constitute layered transparency obligations for third-party AI deployments in EU; India has no equivalent layered transparency framework.
- (Batch 14 — Opacity as the core legal challenge / "black box" doctrine) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F046_UK_LawCommission_AI_and_Law_DiscussionPaper_2025]] — UK Law Commission's Theme 5 (Opacity) is the most detailed doctrinal treatment of AI opacity in the corpus: courts cannot establish causation without understanding AI decisions; Anthropic's own admission ("nobody really knows how they work internally") is cited; UK GDPR Art.22 and Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 s.80 are the current English transparency law. Directly relevant to India's absence of equivalent algorithmic transparency legal rights.
- (Batch 14 — PM-2 model transparency / disclosure to users vs. model internals) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F047_BAIR_Berkeley_Responsible_GenAI_Playbook_2025]] — BAIR's PM-2 play (model card, training data disclosure) operationalises output/process transparency; Transparency risk is one of five key responsibility risks; the playbook distinguishes output transparency (disclosure to users) from model transparency (explainability) — users understand what AI did but not why.
- (Batch 14 — Information Sharing domain / non-contingent disclosure) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F048_FLI_AI_Safety_Index_Winter_2025]] — FLI's Information Sharing domain (3 new indicators in Winter 2025) reveals universally low scores across all 8 companies; no company has robust non-contingent disclosure of safety evaluation findings; Anthropic's shift to default-on user training data is characterised as a transparency regression. The "foundational hypocrisy" finding is itself a transparency failure: companies publicly acknowledge catastrophic risk while privately not investing in safety infrastructure.
- (Batch 15 — Risk Accounting as forward-looking transparency) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Butler_Brooks_Operational_Risk_Paradigm_Change_2024]] (F049) — forward-looking transparency: Risk Accounting as proposed is fundamentally a transparency mechanism. Standardised Risk Units (RUs) enable cross-firm comparability, external scrutiny, and regulator-accessible forward-looking risk transparency, replacing backward-looking capital modelling.
- (Batch 15 — Explainability, communication, and traceability) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Papagiannidis_Mikalef_Conboy_RAI_Governance_Framework_2025]] (F050) — transparency operationalisation: the paper provides a granular operationalisation of transparency in the RAI context, dividing it into explainability (technical process intelligibility), communication (right to know when interacting with AI), and traceability (documentation chain across data and algorithms).
- (Batch 15 continuation — Track A/B as deliberative vs procedural transparency) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/RAND_GovAI_AI_Incident_Reporting_Institutional_Design]] (F051) — Wei/Anderljung/Shevlane/Siegmann: the two-track AIIRS architecture maps directly onto a deliberative vs. procedural transparency distinction. Track A (mandatory/attributed/public database) is procedural transparency — the incident is disclosed to the public and regulators. Track B (voluntary/non-attributed/immunity-protected) is deliberative transparency — findings are aggregated for pattern-learning without individual attribution. Different transparency modes serve different governance goals; conflating them suppresses learning.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Right to explanation as transparency obligation) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/FRA_EU_Assessing_High_Risk_AI_Fundamental_Rights]] (F053) — FRA FRIA guide: EU AI Act Art. 85-86 right to explanation is treated as a transparency obligation grounded in the right to effective remedy — affected individuals must be able to understand AI-assisted decisions to contest them. FRIA documentation and its maintenance is itself a transparency instrument (the assessment record is accessible for regulatory review).
- (Batch 15 continuation — Explainability + monitoring as BFSI transparency operationalisation) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/MindForge_MAS_AI_Risk_Management_Executive_Handbook]] (F054) — MindForge C9 (explainability requirements for AI-assisted financial advice) + C12 (deployment monitoring including hallucination-rate tracking and anomalous-output alerting) are transparency operationalisations in BFSI. C9 is user/customer-facing transparency (AI conduct standards match human advisor standards). C12 is regulator- and internal-audit-facing transparency (monitoring dashboards enable oversight). Together they instantiate transparency across customer and supervisory interfaces.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Domain IV transparency competency) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/IAPP_AIGP_Body_of_Knowledge_v21_2026]] (F055) — Domain IV includes explicit transparency and explainability obligations module: right to explanation, recourse mechanisms, contestability. AIGP-competency-level transparency: practitioners must understand transparency as a legally enforceable competency, not merely a design principle. Provides the practitioner-consensus taxonomy for transparency governance.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Government AIIA transparency architecture) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_20_Australia_DTA_AI_Impact_Assessment_Tool]] — AIIA Tool S8 (transparency/explainability section) mandates ability to explain decisions to affected individuals; proactive public disclosure of High risk AIIAs is a systemic transparency mechanism enabling external scrutiny. This is a **government-level proactive disclosure** transparency mode — distinct from consumer-right-to-explanation (EU) and inventory-reporting (SEBI). India has no equivalent proactive disclosure requirement for government AI systems.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-15 — Disclosed methodology as a transparency model) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F076_CeRAI_IITM_Biased_LMs_Academic_Recommendations_2025]] — CeRAI/IIT Madras's fully disclosed evaluation methodology (hyperparameters, compute, prompt templates, DRS/GRS formulae) models research-transparency practice for LLM fairness auditing; the paper's own recommendation that deployers "compare new model versions against fairness baselines before deployment" treats transparency of testing methodology as a precondition for accountable AI deployment, not merely disclosure of outputs.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-15 — Foundational model-documentation-standard, closes a citation gap) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F080_Mitchell_et_al_Model_Cards_for_Model_Reporting_2019]] — Mitchell et al. (FAT* 2019)'s nine-section Model Card template (Model Details, Intended Use, Factors, Metrics, Evaluation Data, Training Data, Quantitative Analyses, Ethical Considerations, Caveats and Recommendations) is the foundational transparency instrument the vault's own International_20 (Australia AIIA Tool) cites generically as "System factsheet/model card" without ever naming its source. The paper's explicit framing of model cards as "one transparency tool among many" — alongside third-party audit (F041 AEF-1) and adversarial testing — situates documentation-based transparency within the corpus's broader accountability ecosystem rather than treating it as sufficient on its own.
- (Ad hoc, 2026-07-15 — Data-side companion to F080, closes the other half of the citation gap) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F081_Gebru_et_al_Datasheets_for_Datasets_2018]] — Gebru et al. (CACM 2021/arXiv 2018)'s seven-stage dataset documentation questionnaire (Motivation, Composition, Collection Process, Preprocessing, Uses, Distribution, Maintenance) is the data-side counterpart to F080's model-side documentation standard, together completing the "model card + datasheet" pair International_20 references without sourcing. The paper's own explicit caveat — "datasheets for datasets do not provide a complete solution to mitigating unwanted societal biases" — mirrors F080's framing and reinforces documentation transparency as necessary but not sufficient.
- (2026-07-16 — Domain-specific audit-documentation extension of F080/F081, plus validity-as-transparency-target) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F123_Sloane_Moss_Chowdhury_Hiring_Algorithms_Auditability_2021|F123]] — Sloane, Moss & Chowdhury's seven-element socio-technical matrix for hiring algorithmic decision systems explicitly cites F080 (Model Cards) and F081 (Datasheets for Datasets) as its design lineage, extending general-purpose model/dataset documentation into a domain-specific (hiring) audit application. Also introduces a transparency dimension not previously named in the corpus's typology: **epistemological-roots transparency** — audits must interrogate not merely output distribution (demographic parity) but the underlying validity claim of what an ADS purports to measure (e.g., inferring "cultural fit" or emotional state from facial expressions), citing the contested-validity OCEAN Big-5 personality model as a worked example. A sixth transparency type, alongside the corpus's existing regulator-facing/user-facing/market-integrity/systemic-stability/evaluation-process types.
