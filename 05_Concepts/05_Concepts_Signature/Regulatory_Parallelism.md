# **Regulatory Parallelism**

**Type:** Signature Construct**Status:** Active**Last Updated:** 2026-06-27 (Batch 15 instances added)

---

## **Definition (Researcher’s Formulation)**

Regulatory Parallelism is a structurally distinct coordination pattern in which functionally equivalent mandates are issued by independent regulators governing the same systems without cross-reference between their instruments. It differs from convergence because equivalent obligations emerge without a coordination mechanism linking the issuing authorities, and it differs from contradiction because the obligations are duplicative rather than conflicting. The construct identifies a form of regulatory overlap that transfers compliance burden to regulated entities while generating no corresponding coordination dividend within the regulatory architecture itself.

## **Distinction From Adjacent Constructs**

|**Construct**|**Defining Feature**|**Key Difference From Parallelism**|
|---|---|---|
|Convergence|Regulators coordinate to produce aligned requirements|Parallelism lacks the coordination mechanism; equivalence is coincidental not designed|
|Contradiction|Regulators issue conflicting or incompatible requirements|Parallelism involves equivalent not conflicting obligations; the problem is duplication not conflict|
|Regulatory Fragmentation|Absence of regulatory coverage across a domain|Parallelism involves overlapping coverage not absence; the problem is over-specification not under-specification|

## **Boundary Condition — parallelism is subject-matter-specific, not capacity-determined (added 2026-08-02, DEPA sourcing pass)**

The construct's most obvious rebuttal is that Indian regulators produce parallel instruments because they lack the machinery to coordinate. [[06_Frameworks/DEPA]] defeats that rebuttal directly.

RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA and the Ministry of Finance **jointly co-authored** the DEPA framework (NITI Aayog, Aug 2020). Their four ombudsman schemes jointly constitute tier 3 of the Account Aggregator grievance chain, operating as a single redress route across four regulatory perimeters without a coordinating statute (see [[07_Institutions/Corpus/Sahamati/Sahamati_1_SRO_Recognition_and_Grievance_Architecture_2026]]). Technical specification is delegated to a single body with a regulator concurrence gate, versioned change control, and 30-day ecosystem-wide adoption reporting ([[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_12_ReBIT_AA_API_Specifications_2023]]). That is a coordinated, interoperable, multi-regulator architecture — the clearest instance of successful cross-regulator coordination in the entire PRIS corpus.

**The same four regulators, in the same period, produce AI instruments with zero cross-references.**

This is a within-institution control. It holds the institutions, the jurisdiction and the time period constant and varies only the subject matter, which isolates the parallelism variable to **what is being governed** rather than **who is governing**. Two consequences:

1. **Strengthens the construct.** Parallelism cannot be explained away as institutional incapacity; these bodies coordinate when they choose to. The question becomes why AI was not chosen — a governance-priority and problem-framing question, not a capacity one. Connects to [[Governance_Capacity]], which should be read as *deployed* rather than *possessed* capacity.
2. **Bounds the construct.** Parallelism is not a general property of Indian financial-sector regulation. It is a property of Indian financial-sector regulation *of AI*. Claims should be scoped accordingly; an unscoped claim is falsifiable by the DEPA case and a reviewer familiar with India Stack will know it.

Related: [[Proportionality]] supplies the complementary distinction — differential obligations across regulators are defensible where risks genuinely differ and incoherent where they do not. Parallelism proper is the latter.

## **Inter-Branch Parallelism — new structural variant (2026-08-02)**

Every instance recorded to date operates between regulators, or (since RBI_8) within one regulator. [[07_Institutions/Corpus/DHC/DHC_1_ANI_v_OpenAI_2026]] supplies a third structural type: **parallelism between branches of government**.

[[07_Institutions/Corpus/DPIIT/DPIIT_1]] records the DPIIT working paper consulting on whether India should legislate a text-and-data-mining exception, with Nasscom formally dissenting in favour of the TDM model. While that executive consultation was running, the Delhi High Court held on 24 July 2026 that the **existing** s.52(1)(a)(i) of the Copyright Act 1957 already covers LLM training, by giving "research" an updating construction extending it to machine learning. Two branches produced answers to one question, on different constitutional footings, neither citing the other.

| | Inter-regulator | Intra-regulator | **Inter-branch** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Actors | Two agencies of equal type | One agency, two tracks | Executive and judiciary |
| Mechanism | Non-coordination | Deontic placement | Different constitutional function |
| Outputs | Cumulative — entity complies with both | Cumulative | **Potentially substitutive** — a legislated TDM exception may now be redundant, or may be drafted to displace the judicial reading |
| Remediable by coordination? | Yes, in principle | Yes, by drafting discipline | **No** — a court seized of a live dispute cannot defer to a consultation, and the legislature cannot be bound by an interim order |

The final row carries the weight. Prior instances are coordination failures and could in principle be fixed by coordinating. Inter-branch parallelism is a **structural feature of constitutional design rather than a defect**. Para 4 of the judgment states the posture plainly: *"the Courts are required to step in to fill the gap between advancing technologies and existing laws."*

This materially extends the construct's theoretical reach — it can no longer be characterised as a critique of Indian regulatory coordination habits alone.

⚠ The judgment is interim and appealable (⚠ V-DHC1-1). If reversed, the substitutive-output claim weakens; the structural observation survives.

## **Empirical Evidence Base**

The construct emerges from analysis of eighteen binding regulatory instruments issued by RBI, SEBI, and CERT-In between 2012 and 2024. The corpus comprised 492 pages, 450 extracted requirement units, and 91 AI-relevant requirement units subjected to detailed coding and coordination analysis. Across forty coordination pairs distributed across ten governance clusters, twenty-three instances of Parallelism were identified, compared with ten instances of Convergence and one instance of Contradiction. The most significant finding concerned behavioural surveillance obligations, where eleven requirement units across four separate instruments imposed equivalent requirements despite the complete absence of cross-references between RBI and SEBI. Intercoder review achieved a 90 percent agreement rate, with only two of twenty-three edge cases requiring revision, providing confidence that Parallelism represents a stable and observable coordination pattern rather than a coding artefact.

## **Operationalisation**

A coordination pair is classified as Regulatory Parallelism when requirement units issued by different regulators govern the same behavioural domain. The requirement units must impose functionally equivalent obligations on the regulated entity rather than merely addressing related governance concerns. No cross-reference may exist between the instruments at the point where the obligation is imposed, demonstrating the absence of an explicit coordination mechanism. Where these conditions are satisfied, the resulting compliance burden is duplicated at the enterprise level rather than rationalised through regulatory coordination.

## **Theoretical Positioning**

Regulatory Parallelism extends regime complexity theory by identifying a third coordination outcome alongside convergence and contradiction within domestic multi-regulator environments. The construct demonstrates that overlapping regulatory authority does not necessarily produce either alignment or conflict, but may instead generate independent yet equivalent obligations. It engages with polycentric governance theory by showing that polycentric arrangements create coordination costs that are experienced primarily by regulated entities rather than by regulators themselves. The construct also contributes to Enterprise Information Management scholarship by identifying mandate architecture, rather than technology design or organisational capability, as an independent source of enterprise information burden. In doing so, it shifts analytical attention from compliance execution to the structure of the governance environment generating compliance obligations.

## **Downstream Consequence**

Regulatory Parallelism functions as the primary upstream cause of [[Governance_Debt]]. The causal mechanism operates through the sequence of parallel mandates, compliance duplication, and the accumulation of redundant enterprise information management processes, controls, attestations, and reporting structures. As Parallelism increases, governance architectures become less coherent, thereby weakening [[Institutional_Coherence]] and increasing the burden transferred to regulated enterprises.

India's AI governance now demonstrates five independent sector-level silos: BFSI (RBI/SEBI), telecom (TRAI), healthcare ethics (ICMR), healthcare strategy (MoHFW), sub-national state (TN/Telangana/Karnataka).
- (Batch 12 — "Principles-and-existing-law" cluster, 4th confirmed member) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_16_Australia_National_AI_Plan_2025]] — Australia National AI Plan (2025): a fourth primary-source named member of the "principles-and-existing-law" configuration cluster (Australia / Japan / India / US-pre-2025). Australia's "principles before rules" posture (no dedicated AI regulator; existing sectoral law applied; AI Safety Standard voluntary) is analytically equivalent to India's advisory framework (NITI_7 / MeitY_2) and Japan's framework law (F031). Extends the cluster from an editorial typology to a four-jurisdiction evidence base with documented primary sources.
- (Batch 12 — Global South voluntary convergence / non-binding multilateral) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_17_G20_South_Africa_Leaders_Declaration_2025]] — G20 South Africa Leaders Declaration 2025 (AI chapters): multilateral soft-law convergence on AI development principles across 20 major economies, with no enforcement mechanism and no coordination mandate. The G20 SA outcome document endorses existing bodies (UNESCO / GPAI / OECD) without creating a hierarchical integration mechanism — a Regulatory_Parallelism instance at the inter-multilateral-body layer, where multiple international bodies hold overlapping AI governance mandates without a forced integration mechanism.
- (Batch 12 — Global normative parallelism / principle-without-enforcement at 193-state scale) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_18_UNESCO_Recommendation_Ethics_AI_2021]] — UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021): 193-state adoption of an 11-thematic-area AI ethics framework with zero binding enforcement — the corpus's sharpest and most widely ratified instance of principle-adoption without a coordination or enforcement mechanism. Extends Regulatory_Parallelism from the domestic multi-regulator context to the global normative architecture: all 193 UNESCO member states have notionally "adopted" the same principles; no jurisdiction has been held to an accountability standard by a supranational authority for non-implementation.
- (Batch 12 — International accountability-attribution parallelism, strongest three-way instance, confirmed) [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_19_IOSCO_AI_Capital_Markets_CR_2025]] — IOSCO AI in Capital Markets CR/01/2025 (IOSCOPD788, source confirmed 2026-06-19): IOSCO's non-delegable accountability principle — regulated entities retain sole responsibility for AI outputs regardless of third-party vendor — is the global securities regulatory standard. Combined with SEBI_14 (February 2025) and RBI_7 (May 2025), the corpus now documents a three-way convergence on this accountability architecture across global standard-setter and two major Indian regulators within 12 months. SEBI is an IOSCO AIWG member (confirmed, footnote 1, p. 5), yet SEBI_14's sole-responsibility clause predates the IOSCO CR and contains no IOSCO citation. The strongest three-way international-to-domestic Regulatory_Parallelism instance in the corpus.
- (Batch 12 — Japan multi-silo / "apply existing regulation" posture) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F031_Japan_AI_Chambers_Guide_2025]] — Japan AI Law & Practice (Chambers 2025): Japan's multi-silo architecture (METI / MIC / FSA / MHLW / PMDA) directly parallels India's multi-silo (RBI / SEBI / MeitY / TRAI / ICMR). FSA's "apply existing financial regulation to AI" posture (no AI-specific financial regulation as of 2025) is analytically identical to RBI_4's 2017 "apply existing IT governance to AI" posture — a cross-jurisdictional parallelism pair (Japan FSA / India RBI-2017) establishing "applying existing regulation" as a cross-national governance pattern, not an India-specific limitation.
- (Batch 12 — Regime complex theory / theoretical anchor for international persistence) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F032_Maas_Architectures_Global_AI_Governance_2025]] — Maas, *Architectures of Global AI Governance* (OUP 2025): regime complex theory provides the theoretical explanation for why Regulatory_Parallelism persists at the international level — absent a hierarchical authority, governance obligations accumulate across international bodies (OECD / UNESCO / GPAI / IOSCO / BIS / FSB) without a forced integration mechanism. Four governance architecture archetypes (network / forum shopping / hierarchical / polycentric) frame India's domestic architecture as "polycentric-without-coordination," generating Regulatory_Parallelism structurally rather than through regulatory choice.
- (Batch 13 — Cross-jurisdictional evaluation governance convergence / AEF-1 + EU CoP + California SB 53) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F041_AEF1_Minimum_Operating_Conditions_AI_Evaluations]] — AEF-1 (December 2025) is independently cited in EU AI Act Code of Practice (Measure 7.3(1)(g)) and California SB 53 (§22757.12(c)(2)(C)) within the same period, demonstrating convergent cross-jurisdictional adoption of a voluntary standard without formal harmonisation. A Regulatory_Parallelism instance operating in the constructive direction: two regulatory instruments independently converging on the same third-party evaluation independence standard signals the emergence of a cross-jurisdictional norm in evaluation governance, analogous to the IOSCO+SEBI_14+RBI_7 accountability-attribution three-way convergence (Batch 12).
- (Batch 13 — Progressive governance / EU AI Act risk-based approach parallelism) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F037_WEF_Capgemini_AI_Agents_Evaluation_Governance]] — WEF/Capgemini progressive governance (autonomy × authority-scaled oversight) is functionally equivalent to the EU AI Act's risk-based tiering (prohibited → high-risk → limited risk → minimal risk) applied specifically to agentic systems, without direct citation of the EU AI Act in the governance framework section. A practitioner-to-regulatory convergence parallelism signal: multi-stakeholder (WEF) and regulatory (EU) frameworks independently arrive at risk-proportionate oversight as the foundational agentic AI governance design.
- (Batch 14 — Shadow AI monitoring: four Indian silos, zero coordination) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F042_IBM_Cost_Data_Breach_Report_2025]] — IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025: Shadow AI (unauthorised AI tool deployment) generating a USD 670K per-breach premium creates a governance obligation vacuum that India's four regulatory silos (RBI/SEBI/MeitY/IRDAI) have not filled through any coordinated Shadow AI monitoring standard. RBI CSCRF addresses cybersecurity incident response; SEBI AI guidelines address accountability for registered AI systems; MeitY IndiaAI Guidelines address development principles; IRDAI addresses data governance — none coordinates on ungoverned/Shadow AI monitoring. Each silo's entity population faces the Shadow AI risk without a coordinated regulatory response — a Shadow AI monitoring governance fragmentation instance extending the BFSI Regulatory_Parallelism pattern beyond model governance into the Shadow AI layer.
- (Batch 14 — EU Digital Decade regulatory fragmentation / "Good TPRM" compliance burden as Parallelism) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F045_PwC_TPRM_Digital_Geopolitical_Era_2025]] — PwC Netherlands TPRM in the Digital and Geopolitical Era (November 2025): "Regulatory Fragmentation" (sixth TPRM pitfall) — firms in the EU must comply in parallel with DORA (January 2025), NIS2 (October 2024), DSA (February 2024), and AI Act (2024/2026) without an integrated compliance framework. Regulatory Parallelism operating within the EU's multi-instrument TPRM layer: the EU has a common market and shared institutional architecture, yet firms face parallel obligations from four co-existing TPRM instruments. India's inverse: RBI_2 IT Outsourcing, RBI FREE-AI, SEBI AI circulars, and DPDP Act impose TPRM-adjacent obligations without either a common framework or integration mechanism — regulatory fragmentation without the EU's underlying coherence architecture. PwC's "Great TPRM" integrated solution is the enterprise-level anti-parallelism response the EU incentivises; no equivalent Indian incentive exists.
- (Batch 14 — Developer-level governance parallelism / three uncoordinated regimes for the same 8 companies) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F048_FLI_AI_Safety_Index_Winter_2025]] — FLI AI Safety Index Winter 2025 (December 2025): frontier AI companies face three simultaneous, uncoordinated governance accountability regimes — EU AI Code of Practice (mandatory for GPAI providers from August 2025), US voluntary commitments (White House commitments, no binding instrument), and Chinese mandatory reporting (GB45438-2025 watermarking; Deep Synthesis Regulations; Interim Measures for GenAI). All three apply to the same eight frontier AI companies across the same capability development activities without mutual recognition or coordination. Regulatory Parallelism at the developer-governance layer — the most upstream instance in the corpus (developer-level, not deployer- or regulator-level), and the first corpus instance where Chinese mandatory regulation and Western voluntary governance operate as genuinely parallel frameworks generating different compliance outcomes for the same entities.
- (Batch 15 — Second-order RP mitigation at enterprise layer) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Butler_Brooks_Operational_Risk_Paradigm_Change_2024]] (F049) — indirect relevance: the proposed AI-enabled Enterprise Data Fabric and RACE architecture act as a second-order RP mitigation mechanism; a firm that integrates risk data across the enterprise is better positioned to produce coherent, consistent regulatory reporting to multiple overlapping regulators simultaneously.
- (Batch 15 — Multi-regulatory complexity gap) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Papagiannidis_Mikalef_Conboy_RAI_Governance_Framework_2025]] (F050) — firm-level limitation: the framework's focus on intra-organisational governance highlights the multi-regulatory complexity gap for P3; in real-world BFSI environments like India, firms must navigate structural/procedural/relational requirements under multiple simultaneous, uncoordinated regulatory mandates (RBI + SEBI + IRDAI + MeitY).
- (Batch 15 continuation — Multi-actor AIIRS reporting scope as RP-compounding) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/RAND_GovAI_AI_Incident_Reporting_Institutional_Design]] (F051) — Wei/Anderljung/Shevlane/Siegmann: AIIRS Dimension 2 (Reporter scope: developer/deployer/user/third-party auditor) intersects Regulatory Parallelism at the reporting layer — multi-actor reporting scope across overlapping regulators compounds RP risk. Single-window AIIRS architecture (one national safety analysis body receiving all reports) is a partial RP mitigation; multi-window architecture (each regulator maintains its own reporting system) is an RP amplification. India currently has multi-window Track A reporting (DPDP breach notification / RBI cyber / CERT-In direction / sector-specific) with zero coordination — the RP-amplification configuration.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Layer-boundary RP at compute/model/application) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Paladin_GI_AI_Tech_Stack_Primer]] (F052) — Walden & Lynch: compute/model/application layer governance involves different ministries and agencies (Commerce for export controls, safety agencies for model licensing, sector regulators for application governance). RP risk is highest at layer boundaries — where does compute regulation end and model regulation begin? Where does model regulation end and application regulation begin? Layer-blind regulation collapses these boundaries; layer-aware regulation demarcates them but creates coordination challenges. India's architecture is layer-blind at the AI-specific layer (no Layer 2 instrument) but layer-specific at the general layer (MeitY vs sectoral regulators) — a hybrid RP configuration.
- (Batch 15 continuation — Parallelism through Omission, RBI vs IRDAI) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Cross_Institutional_Mandate_Comparison]] §6.3 introduces the pattern variant **Parallelism through Omission** — where two functionally comparable sectoral regulators (both financial-sector, both governing information-asymmetric markets) diverge sharply on AI governance not through issuing conflicting rules but through one regulator's *absence* of AI-specific rules while the other builds an elaborate AI governance stack. RBI has built [[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_7]] + [[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_FREE_AI_2025]] + [[07_Institutions/Corpus/RBI/RBI_2]]; [[07_Institutions/Corpus/IRDAI/IRDAI_1_Maintenance_and_Sharing_of_Information_Regulations_2025]] is silent on AI/ML. Insurance companies deploying AI operate in a regulatory vacuum while banks under RBI face highly prescriptive mandates. Parallelism through Omission is analytically distinct from ordinary parallelism because the pattern generates *asymmetric* enterprise burden across sectors rather than duplicative burden within the same sector.
- (Batch 15 continuation — DLT-Privacy Direct Legal Contradiction, TRAI_1 vs MeitY_4) [[04_Knowledge_Products/Cross_Institutional_Mandate_Comparison]] §10.1 documents the sharpest direct-legal-contradiction instance in the corpus: TRAI's [[07_Institutions/Corpus/TRAI/TRAI_1]] UCC Spam Direction 2026 mandates that telecom access providers deploy AI spam detection and share flagged numbers via Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) within 2 hours; MeitY's [[07_Institutions/Corpus/MeitY/MeitY_4_DPDP_Rules_2025]] DPDP Rules 2025 (Rule 8) enforces a strict "right to erasure" and purpose limitation. Because DLT architectures are structurally immutable, a telecom provider cannot erase a flagged number from the ledger without breaking the TRAI mandate. This is Regulatory Parallelism operating in its most severe form — a *direct legal contradiction* between horizontal (privacy) and vertical (telecom) instruments. Feeds directly into [[P4_Doctrinal_IJLIT]] as a standalone doctrinal analysis.

## **🔷 Parallelism Has a Normative Defence: the "Shared AI Governance Floor" (2026-08-09)**

Source: [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_38_World_Bank_WDR2026_AI_Governance]] Ch.9, p. 326, primary text; citing Schüür, Birtwistle, Field Reid, Groves and Rincon (2026), Ada Lovelace Institute.

**This note has treated parallelism as a pathology throughout** — a coordination failure producing duplicated obligation, compliance cost, and [[Governance_Debt]]. That framing is defensible at the intra-jurisdiction scale where the construct was developed. It is **not** the only available position at the international scale, and a World Bank flagship has now stated the alternative:

> *"global dialogue should aim for **regulatory pluralism** — allowing countries to shape national governance frameworks in ways that match local communities' needs — as its goal, but underpinned by a **shared AI governance floor**, where countries agree to ensure a common level of human rights protection and interoperability of different governance regimes."*

**What this does to the construct.** It separates two things this note has bundled:

| | **Divergence in substance** | **Divergence in floor** |
|---|---|---|
| Present | Regulators impose different requirements for the same capability | Regulators disagree on the minimum protection owed |
| Normative status per WDR | **Acceptable — arguably desirable**, because local context differs | **Not acceptable** — this is what produces a race to the bottom |

Parallelism as currently operationalised in this note does not distinguish these. Two regulators requiring different audit formats for the same AI system is substantive divergence with a shared floor. Two regulators disagreeing on whether an audit is owed at all is floor divergence. **The compliance cost of the first is real; the governance failure is only in the second.**

**Why this matters for P1 and P3 specifically.** Any framing that treats *all* parallelism as fragmentation is now vulnerable to the response that the World Bank explicitly endorses pluralism as a goal. The stronger position is to **adopt the distinction and locate the Indian BFSI evidence within it** — and the honest reading is that most Indian instances recorded in this note *are* substantive divergence rather than floor divergence, which makes them a compliance-burden finding rather than a rights-protection finding. That is a narrower claim, and it is the one the evidence actually supports.

**The floor is not currently reachable, and the report supplies the reason.** WDR Figure 9.6: of seven non-UN interregional AI governance initiatives, **seven countries — all high income — are party to all; 118 countries are party to none**; 48 of 54 African Group members are involved in none. Only UNESCO's Recommendation was developed with broad developing-country participation. **A floor negotiated by seven countries is not a floor.** The participation asymmetry is the binding constraint on the report's own proposed remedy — which is a tension the report does not resolve, and therefore a legitimate opening for critical engagement.

**Related:** [[Institutional_Coherence]] — the floor concept is a coherence mechanism operating at the international rather than intra-state scale. [[06_Frameworks/ISO_IEC_42001]] — the standards channel through which a floor would in practice be built, and where the same participation asymmetry recurs (one-third developing-country membership on ISO/IEC AI committees).

## **Future Research and Reuse Opportunities**

- **Operationalise the substantive-divergence / floor-divergence distinction** introduced above, and recode the existing Indian instances against it. This is a bounded, high-value task and it materially sharpens what the construct claims.
- Cross-sector replication studies examining whether Regulatory Parallelism appears in healthcare AI governance, defence procurement governance, and Digital Public Infrastructure governance.
- International comparative analysis assessing whether Parallelism emerges within dual-regulated sectors across the European Union, United Kingdom, Singapore, and other multi-regulator jurisdictions.
- Executive and graduate teaching cases using Indian BFSI governance as an archetypal example of Parallelism in practice.
- Development of a consulting diagnostic framework enabling BFSI organisations to identify, map, and manage Parallelism within their regulatory obligations.
