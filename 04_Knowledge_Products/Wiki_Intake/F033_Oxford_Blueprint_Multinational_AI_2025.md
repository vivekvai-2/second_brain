## 3–5 Reusable Findings

1. **Global AI compute concentration — structural governance problem** (feeds S1/S3): The Blueprint quantifies the AI compute concentration problem: the United States controls approximately 75% of global AI compute capacity, China 15%, and the EU 5%. All other nations — including India — operate in the remaining 5%. This structural asymmetry is the material basis for the Blueprint's governance proposal: "bridge powers" (EU + allied mid-sized tech democracies) must create a multinational development alternative to avoid permanent dependence on US or Chinese frontier AI systems. For PRIS: this compute concentration data contextualises India's sovereign AI framing (NITI_8 DeepSeek analysis) and NCAIC_1's proposed coordination role — India's governance aspirations are structurally constrained by 5% compute access.

2. **Multinational development consortium — the CERN/Airbus model for AI** (feeds S1/S3): The Blueprint proposes a voluntary multinational partnership for frontier AI development, drawing on precedents from CERN (physics) and Airbus (aerospace). The consortium would enable member nations to: preserve AI development sovereignty; pool compute and pre-training resources; share safety research; lead through ethical stewardship of AI systems. The core governance mechanism proposed is **pooled pre-training** (shared foundation model development) with sovereign fine-tuning rights retained by each member. This is the most concrete multilateral AI development governance proposal in the corpus — more operationally specific than G20 SA's "AI for Africa" voluntary cooperation.

3. **"Bridge powers" as the critical governance variable** (feeds S3): The Blueprint introduces "bridge powers" as a category distinct from US/China AI superpowers and from AI-importing developing countries. Bridge powers are mid-sized tech democracies (EU member states, UK, Canada, Australia, South Korea, Japan, India — implicitly) with: strong data protection regimes; robust rule of law; significant tech talent pools; but insufficient compute scale to compete individually at the AI frontier. The Blueprint argues bridge powers have a structural advantage in *reliable* AI (not scale AI) — high-value sectors (healthcare, legal, finance, defence adjacent) require control and reliability before scale. This frames India's governance regime (DPDP + MeitY AI Guidelines + RBI FREE-AI) as a structural asset for "bridge power" participation — precisely because India's principles-based approach creates predictable regulatory environments for reliable AI deployment.

4. **Reliability AI as the unmet market need for bridge powers** (feeds S1/S3): The Blueprint's key strategic insight: "building reliable AI represents an unmet market need where bridge powers have structural advantages." High-value regulated sectors (BFSI, healthcare, defence-adjacent) cannot deploy AI at scale without confidence in explainability, accountability, and governance. The US/China frontier competition is dominated by scale (GPT-5, Gemini, DeepSeek) — but regulated sectors need governance-certified AI, not just capable AI. For PRIS/P3: this is the theoretical market-structure explanation for why BFSI-specific AI governance (RBI FREE-AI, SEBI_14, IOSCO CR, International_19) is commercially significant — it creates the regulatory environment that enables reliable AI deployment in capital-intensive regulated sectors.

5. **International AI governance coordination — the MAGIC vs. GPAI debate** (feeds S1/S3): The Blueprint engages with the existing international AI governance landscape (GPAI, G7 Hiroshima AI Process, G20 AI Task Force, AI Safety Summits) and argues these are insufficient for frontier development governance because they address principles without addressing the structural question of *who builds* the next generation of AI systems. The Blueprint's consortium proposal is explicitly positioned as a governance architecture supplement to, not replacement of, existing principle frameworks — filling the "production governance gap" between UNESCO/OECD principles (what AI should do) and actual development decisions (who builds, how, under what constraints).
