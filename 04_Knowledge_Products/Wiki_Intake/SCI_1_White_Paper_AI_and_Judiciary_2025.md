# SCI_1 — White Paper on Artificial Intelligence and Judiciary

## Header

Published by the Centre for Research and Planning (CRP), Supreme Court of India, November 2025 (© Supreme Court of India, 2025). Authored by Dr. Anurag Bhaskar (Director-cum-Additional Registrar, CRP) and a team of CRP consultants/law clerks. 81 pages across an introduction, six substantive chapters (AI definitions; global comparative survey of 13 jurisdictions plus UNESCO/OECD/EU frameworks; India's e-Courts and Supreme Court AI tools; six categories of AI risk; six core ethical principles; and institutional recommendations/guidelines for courts, lawyers, and law clerks).

## Classification

This is the corpus's **first instrument issued by the judiciary itself** (as distinct from executive ministries — MeitY, RBI, SEBI, IRDAI — or statutory bodies — CERT-In, NCAIC) addressing AI governance. It is simultaneously (a) a comparative-law reference work (Chapter 2's 13-jurisdiction survey is encyclopedic and citation-rich), (b) an institutional self-audit (Chapter 3 catalogues India's own AI tools — SUPACE, SUVAS, TERES, LegRAA, AI Saransh, Adalat AI), and (c) a soft-law guidance instrument (Chapters 5–6 set out ethical principles and conduct guidelines for judges, lawyers, and law clerks — the first corpus document to address law clerks/research associates as a distinct regulated category). ⚠VERIFY — the White Paper is explicitly framed as recommendatory/advisory ("suggestive guidelines"), not binding rules; its normative weight derives from institutional authorship (Supreme Court's own research centre) rather than any rule-making power. Treat as soft law / persuasive institutional guidance, not subordinate legislation.

## Reusable Findings

**1. India's AI-in-judiciary toolkit is now a named, catalogued ecosystem with at least nine operative or pilot tools.** The White Paper provides the corpus's most complete inventory of Indian judicial AI deployments: SUPACE (ML-based case-record analysis for judges), SUVAS (rule-based translation engine — 36,000 SC judgments translated into 19 languages in 2023 alone), TERES (real-time AI transcription for Constitution Bench hearings, also deployed at Delhi HC), LegRAA (GenAI-based legal research/brief-generation tool drawing on a 36,000-judgment corpus), AI Saransh (NIC-developed pleadings précis tool), Adalat AI (Kerala HC-mandated deposition transcription), the IIT-Madras e-filing defect-detection pilot, NyayKaushal (virtual court/e-resource centre), and NALSA's LESA chatbot. ⚠VERIFY — several of these (LegRAA, AI Saransh, the IIT-Madras pilot) are described as "being developed"/"pilot" rather than fully operational; treat operational-status claims as provisional pending primary confirmation, especially for any claims of system-wide deployment.

**2. The White Paper documents three confirmed Indian judicial AI-hallucination incidents, providing the corpus's first judiciary-acknowledged catalogue of generative-AI failure modes in Indian courts.** These are: (i) a Karnataka trial court judge whose GenAI-drafted judgment portions contained fabricated citations and non-existent precedents (Karnataka HC proposed action against the judge, March 2025); (ii) an Income Tax Appellate Tribunal order recalled after reliance on "fictitious" judicial precedents (February 2025); and (iii) a Delhi HC case in which a lawyer's ChatGPT-drafted plea contained fabricated quotations attributed to non-existent cases (August 2023). The White Paper situates these alongside the well-known US cases (*Coomer v. Lindell*, *Mata v. Avianca*) and the Stanford RegLab finding that leading legal-AI research tools (Lexis+ AI, Ask Practical Law, WestLaw AI-Assisted Research) hallucinate at rates of 17–34%. This is directly relevant to any P1/P3 discussion of AI hallucination as a documented, jurisdiction-specific governance failure mode rather than a hypothetical risk — and is the corpus's strongest evidentiary basis for "human-in-the-loop" mandates appearing across other instruments (cf. NCAIC_1's evaluation gates, CERT_In_1's audit-verification chain).

**3. The White Paper's six-category risk taxonomy and six-principle ethics framework provide a reusable structural template that closely parallels — and could be cross-mapped against — NCAIC_1's risk-tiering and CERT_In_1's audit-principle structure.** Risks identified: (A) overreliance/diminished human judgement, (B) fabrication/hallucination, (C) evidence tampering/deepfakes, (D) algorithmic bias and discrimination, (E) IP infringement, (F) breach of confidentiality/privacy. Ethical principles: human-in-the-loop, accuracy and verification, confidentiality and privacy, fairness and bias prevention, specialised verification of translated texts, and restricted use in administrative/routine functions only. The pairing of a risk taxonomy with a corresponding principles framework — and the document's own "red flag indicators of AI-generated work" checklist (Chapter 6) — is a strong candidate for Institutional Grammar 2.0 / ADICO coding given its explicit, enumerated, role-differentiated obligations (separate guideline sets for judicial institutions, lawyers, and law clerks).

**4. The White Paper is the first corpus document to propose role-differentiated AI governance obligations for three distinct judicial-system actor categories — institutions, lawyers, and law clerks/research associates — each with its own guideline set (Chapter 6, Sections C–E).** The law-clerk guidelines (Section E, drawing on the Supreme Court's "Handbook on Law Clerks cum Research Associate") are notably the first instance in the corpus of AI governance extending to a junior-research-staff category, with explicit reference to "professional misconduct" and disciplinary consequences for unethical AI use. This role-differentiation (institution / senior practitioner / junior research staff) is a useful comparative axis against NCAIC_1's RACI-based governance-role model and CERT_In_1's maker-checker/three-tier sign-off structure — three different institutional approaches to distributing AI-related accountability across hierarchical actor categories.

**5. The global comparative survey (Chapter 2) functions as a ready-made literature map for P1's cross-jurisdictional AI-and-courts analysis, covering 13 jurisdictions plus three international bodies (UNESCO, OECD, EU) with primary-source citations to each jurisdiction's governing instrument.** Notable comparative data points directly useful for fsQCA configurational coding: UNESCO's 2023 survey found 44% of 563 judicial operators across 96 countries had used AI tools, with 71% using "free access" (non-enterprise) versions — a striking data-security baseline. The EU AI Act's four-tier risk classification, Brazil's Bill No. 2338/2023 (excessive/high/low risk categories with judiciary AI classified as high-risk), and the US's fragmented state-by-state/court-by-court approach (no federal AI-in-courts statute) provide three distinct regulatory-architecture archetypes (horizontal statute / risk-tiered bill / fragmented soft law) that map onto the "amendment-based vs. horizontal-statute" variable already identified via MeitY_5.

## Consulting / Teaching Reuse

**AEGIS_OS**: The White Paper's institutional-safeguards framework (AI Ethics Committees within courts, in-house AI tool development, Ethical Use of AI Policy template, disclosure/audit-trail requirements, and the "red flag indicators of AI-generated work" checklist) is directly transferable to any AEGIS_OS engagement involving AI-governance policy design for judicial, quasi-judicial (tribunals), or large professional-services (law firm) clients — the lawyer and law-clerk guideline sets (Sections D–E) could be adapted nearly verbatim as a starting template for a law-firm AI-use policy. The hallucination-incident catalogue (Finding 2) is strong illustrative material for CISO/GC-facing practitioner content on generative-AI risk in regulated professional contexts.

**Teaching/Thought leadership**: The role-differentiated governance model (Finding 4) and the six-risk/six-principle framework (Finding 3) are well-suited to a LinkedIn carousel or practitioner briefing on "AI governance for professional services — lessons from the judiciary," targeting Vivek's governance/risk practitioner audience. The comparative survey (Finding 5) could anchor a teaching module on global AI-and-courts regulatory archetypes for an AI governance course module.
