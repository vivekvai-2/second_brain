## Scope Note — Why This Node Exists

The most conceptually significant addition yet to the P2 records-rules asymmetry cluster — not another corroborating or extending data point, but a genuine **complication**: this paper argues that user-centricity (voice/participation) does not merely need "more of it" to close the asymmetry, but that user-centricity itself structurally *conflicts* with other legitimate public values (representation, pluralism, accountability, inclusiveness). Closing the gap is not simply a design or resourcing problem; it involves real normative trade-offs.

---

## Key Findings and Framework

### Four value conflicts (Table 8) — reframes the entire P2 cluster's normative stance

1. **User focus–representation conflict:** citizens' and governments' interests/needs diverge; governments cannot represent user needs to the full extent user-centricity prescribes ("[governments focus on] fulfilling requirements rather than trying to understand the needs of their users," Kotamraju & van der Geest, 2012).
2. **User focus–pluralism conflict:** no single design approach is optimal across a pluralistic society; designing for one user group's needs can under-serve others.
3. **User involvement–accountability conflict:** active citizen participation can be incompatible with public officials' professional accountability to formal rules — "if tight regulations remain, all... proactiveness of citizens and end users may be of little use or even get nullified" (Ghosh Roy & Upadhyay, 2017).
4. **User involvement–inclusiveness conflict:** citizen involvement via online/collaborative-design channels "often leaves behind those whose voices are most needed" (David, 2018) — participation itself can be exclusionary, since it demands time, resources, digital literacy, and specifically-trained administrators to manage.

**Direct P2 significance:** conflict #4 is a direct, named complication of F110's Cape Town positive-counterexample and F107/F108/F111/F114's asymmetry-closing framing — even well-designed participation channels can reproduce exclusion along different lines (those with time/literacy/resources to participate vs. those without) rather than eliminating it.

### Five conflict sources (Table 8)

Decision-making dominance (power imbalance between experienced decision-makers and other stakeholders); degree of participation (how far citizens can realistically be involved); resource deficit (technical/digital-literacy and financial/infrastructure shortfalls); establishment-innovation issue (novelty-averse bureaucratic structures vs. dynamic innovation); multistakeholder issue (conflicting interests among service-provider organisations and stakeholders).

### Extended public-values taxonomy (Table 1) — directly reusable vocabulary

Three domains (Bannister & Connolly, 2014), extended with ~30 named values: **duty-oriented** (responsibility/political neutrality, compliance with law, accountability to government, rectitude, legitimacy, sustainability); **service-oriented** (efficient/economical use of public funds, service to the citizen, responsiveness, effectiveness, efficiency, transparency, productivity, innovation); **socially-oriented** (facilitating democratic will, representation, inclusiveness, justice, fairness/equity, equality of access, due process, privacy protection, accountability to the public, consultation/participation, pluralism/diversity, trust). A directly reusable, richly-populated values taxonomy for any P2 analysis of which public values India's DPI systems explicitly or implicitly prioritise.

### Methodological transparency note

The authors explicitly flag their conflict-source findings as derived through "abductive reasoning" and state plainly "we cannot speak of statistical causation or correlation" — a citable model of appropriate epistemic humility for qualitative synthesis findings, worth noting alongside the corpus's broader rhetoric-vs-evidence-gap thread (F108, F110).
