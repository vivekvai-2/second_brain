## 3–5 Reusable Findings

1. **Three-facet change framework for global AI governance** (feeds S1/S3): Maas argues that global AI governance must reckon with three simultaneous and interacting dimensions of change: (a) sociotechnical changes in AI systems' capabilities and societal impacts; (b) AI-driven disruptions to the fabric of international law itself (AI may alter treaty interpretation, state responsibility attribution, international dispute resolution); and (c) political changes in the global AI regime complex (shifts in power, coalitions, and governance legitimacy). The value for PRIS is conceptual: this three-facet framework explains why AI governance fragmentation is not simply a coordination-failure problem but a more fundamental transformation of international institutional architecture.

2. **Regime complex analysis — AI governance as overlapping, non-hierarchical institutions** (feeds S1/S3): Drawing from global governance scholarship, Maas characterises AI governance not as a single regime but as a *regime complex* — an array of partially overlapping and non-hierarchical institutions including OECD AI Principles, UNESCO Recommendation, EU AI Act, G7 Hiroshima AI Process, G20 declarations, national AI strategies, and sectoral standards (IOSCO, BIS/FSI, BCBS). The regime complex framing directly explains why [[Regulatory_Parallelism]] persists at the international level — there is no hierarchical governance authority to enforce coherence, only voluntary coordination at various forums. This is the theoretical frame Vivek's P1 SLR is missing for the "international fragmentation" section.

3. **Institutional choice in global AI governance — four architecture archetypes** (feeds S1/S3): Maas identifies four archetypes for global AI governance architecture: (a) *Network governance* (informal, multi-stakeholder, no binding authority — GPAI, AI Safety Summit process); (b) *Forum shopping* (states/firms exploit fragmentation between regimes — the "regulatory arbitrage" problem); (c) *Hierarchical regime* (single dominant authority — aspiration of AI Act extraterritoriality, not yet achieved); (d) *Polycentric governance* (multiple overlapping authorities with mutual recognition — the emerging de facto model). These four archetypes provide P1's comparative taxonomy with theoretical grounding from global governance literature.

4. **Human choice as the central variable** (feeds S1): The subtitle "From Technological Change to Human Choice" reflects Maas's core normative argument: the global AI governance architecture is not technologically determined but is the product of political choices by governments, international institutions, and civil society. The implication for PRIS research: [[Institutional_Coherence]] is not an emergent property of AI technology adoption but a deliberate governance design choice — and its absence in India's multi-regulator BFSI architecture is therefore an institutional decision, not an inevitability.

5. **Disruption of international law — AI and state responsibility** (feeds S3/S1): Maas argues that AI creates novel problems for traditional international law doctrines — particularly attribution of state responsibility when AI systems cause harm, application of jus ad bellum to autonomous weapons, and treaty interpretation under systems that may make text parsing obsolete. For P4 (doctrinal analysis), this provides the international law theoretical frame for the "AI and institutional accountability" gap: if international law struggles with AI attribution, domestic doctrinal frameworks (SEBI sole responsibility clause, RBI LSP accountability chain) are plugging a global institutional gap.
