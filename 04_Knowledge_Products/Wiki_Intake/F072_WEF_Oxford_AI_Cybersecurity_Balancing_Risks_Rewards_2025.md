## Document Summary

A senior-leadership-oriented white paper produced by the WEF AI Governance Alliance (launched June 2023) with Oxford's GCSCC, addressing a single central question: how organisations can capture AI adoption benefits while managing the cybersecurity risks AI introduces. Structured around a step-by-step AI cyber-risk management methodology ("shift left, expand right, and repeat") and a set of governance questions for senior risk owners. Distinct from — and complementary to — F037 (WEF/Capgemini, AI Agents Evaluation and Governance), this is WEF's cybersecurity-specific instalment within the same industry-transformation series, addressing AI cyber risk as a leadership governance problem rather than a technical agent-evaluation problem.

## Core Content

### 1. Eight Foundational Best Practices for Securing AI Systems

Risk-based approach to AI adoption; cross-disciplinary AI risk function (legal, cyber, compliance, technology, risk, HR, ethics, front-line business units); AI application inventory (addressing "shadow AI" and supply-chain risk); disciplined experimentation-to-operational transition especially for mission-critical applications; adequate cybersecurity-control investment for AI system resilience and recovery; combined pre-deployment ("shift left"/security-by-design) and post-deployment ("expand right") controls, repeated throughout the AI lifecycle; people- and process-based controls alongside technical controls; information-governance discipline over what data is exposed to AI systems.

### 2. "Shift Left, Expand Right, and Repeat" Methodology

A four-stage framing of emerging cybersecurity practice for AI: (1) shift left — security-by-design at development stage; (2) shift left and expand right — extending controls to post-deployment monitoring; (3) shift left, expand right, and repeat — iterative reapplication across the AI system lifecycle as models/data/threats evolve; (4) taking an enterprise view — integrating AI cyber risk into overall enterprise risk management rather than siloing it as a standalone AI-specific programme.

### 3. Six Governance Questions for Senior Risk Owners

Has appropriate AI risk tolerance been established and understood by all risk owners? Are risks weighed against rewards for new AI projects? Is there an effective process to govern and track AI project deployment? Is there clear understanding of organisation-specific AI vulnerabilities and cyber risks? Is there clarity on which stakeholders must be involved in assessing/mitigating AI cyber risk? Are assurance processes in place to ensure AI deployments remain consistent with organisational policy and legal/regulatory obligations?

### 4. Risk-Management Step Sequence (Section 4)

Understanding how organisational context shapes AI cyber risk → understanding the rewards → identifying potential risks and vulnerabilities → assessing potential negative business impacts → identifying risk-mitigation options → balancing residual risk against potential rewards → repeating throughout the AI lifecycle. This is a structured, sequential risk-management protocol explicitly designed to be adaptable beyond AI to other emerging technologies.

## Analytical Notes

**Cross-disciplinary AI risk function as an Accountability/Governance_Capacity instrument.** The explicit call for a cross-disciplinary risk function spanning legal, cyber, compliance, technology, risk, HR, and ethics is a more explicit organisational-accountability architecture than most vendor security whitepapers in the corpus (contrast F071 AWS, which is technically deeper but organisationally thinner on the accountability-structure question). This is directly useful for board/CRO-facing consulting collateral on AI governance committee design.

**"Shift left, expand right, repeat" as a lifecycle-governance parallel to regulatory phased-compliance models.** The iterative, lifecycle-repeating structure echoes the phased-implementation logic seen in regulatory instruments in the corpus (e.g., RBI_FREE_AI_2025's sutra-based phased rollout) but originates from a cybersecurity-practice rather than regulatory-compliance starting point — a useful cross-genre parallel for the Institutional_Coherence concept (independent convergence on phased/iterative governance logic across regulatory and industry-practice sources).

**Multi-stakeholder derivation method adds evidentiary weight relative to single-vendor whitepapers.** Because this paper is explicitly derived from structured workshops and interviews across business, government, academia, and civil society (rather than a single company's internal practice, as in F071/F070/F035), its governance recommendations carry a broader consensus/legitimacy claim — relevant to the Legitimacy concept as a corpus instance of multi-stakeholder-derived (rather than unilaterally-asserted) AI governance guidance.
