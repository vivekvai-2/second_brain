## Key Findings and Framework

### Definitional anchor and conceptual-ambiguity gap

Open government is defined as "a new governing structure emphasizing both transparency (vision) and public participation (voice)." Yet of 189 reviewed articles, only **38 (20%) provide a clear definition** of open government at all, and only **10 articles attempt an original definition** — the rest borrow or leave the concept undefined. A further 37 articles (20%) define the narrower "open government data" concept, and **114 articles (60%) proceed without any definition whatsoever**. A striking, quantified conceptual-rigor gap — directly parallel to F107's finding that only 22 of 58 citizen-centricity studies define their core term — reinforcing a pattern across the DPI/open-government literature that key constructs are widely invoked but rarely defined.

### "Vision without voice" — implementation imbalance, direct P2 relevance

Of 153 empirical studies, 114 (75%) focus on technology/data (the "vision"/transparency dimension); only 14 (12%) focus on participation and just 5 (4%) on collaboration (the "voice" dimension). A direct, quantified sharpening of P2's records-rules asymmetry finding: open government implementation in practice systematically over-invests in transparency/data-provision (citizens as passive record-recipients) relative to participation/collaboration mechanisms (citizens as active, rule-shaping actors) — the same asymmetry pattern F107 documents via its User/Governance-vs-Design/End-result necessity finding, now corroborated by a second, independently-sampled 189-study systematic review.

### Rhetoric-vs-evidence gap

Of 75 articles that theorise potential open-government impacts (Table 6: citizen-level effects — participation, trust, empowerment; government-level effects — transparency, accountability, performance; societal effects — economic/social value, democracy), only **8 studies** offer any actual empirical evidence for these hypothesised impacts. A citable rhetoric-vs-evidence gap directly analogous to the unevidenced-benefit-claims pattern PRIS already tracks in AI governance discourse (cf. F077 OpenAI's self-reported figures, ⚠#216) — useful as a cross-domain precedent that governance-technology impact claims routinely outrun their evidence base.

### Multilayer analytical framework (Roberts, 2020) — directly reusable

Macro level (general strategies/conceptualisation of administrative reform) / meso level (consolidation or implementation of reform) / micro level (individual attitudes/behaviour change) — a directly transferable three-tier analytical structure for organising P2's own analysis of India's DPI governance across national-strategy (macro), implementation-architecture (meso), and citizen-behaviour (micro) levels.

### Geographic imbalance

Open government research is concentrated in North America (31.88%) and Europe (26.25%); only 15.63% covers Asia and 3.13% Africa. The paper's own future-research recommendation calls explicitly for more Asia/Africa coverage — a citable positioning point for framing India-focused DPI research (P2) as filling a documented geographic gap in the open-government literature, not merely applying an established Western framework to a new case.
