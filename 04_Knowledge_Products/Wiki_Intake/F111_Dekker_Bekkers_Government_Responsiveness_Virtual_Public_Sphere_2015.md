## Key Findings and Framework

### Three-tier public sphere model — the cluster's missing theoretical vocabulary

Miège's (2010) layered public sphere, building on Fraser's (1992) strong/weak publics distinction: **elite sphere** (top tier — organs of state, legislatures, corporate sector; a "strong" public sphere directly linked to decision-making); **mainstream public sphere** (middle tier — mass media, vested interests, parties); **societal sphere** (lowest tier — "private, unorganized citizens remote from decision-making centers"; a "weak" public sphere). This maps precisely onto the records-rules asymmetry the cluster documents empirically: citizens positioned in the societal/weak-public sphere are exactly F107's "records"/End-result recipients; citizens positioned in the elite/strong-public sphere are F107's Governance-level, rule-shaping actors. Dekker & Bekkers supply the named theoretical apparatus (Fraser's strong/weak publics) the cluster's other three nodes describe only through empirical percentages.

### Participation ≠ responsiveness — a second-order finding for the cluster

Responsiveness is defined precisely as "the congruence of collective public attitudes towards political issues with the policy preferences and actions of elected representatives" (Hobolt & Klemmensen, 2005) — a substantively different, and analytically distinct, concept from the mere existence of a citizen-facing participation channel. Across the 39-study explanatory sample, **institutional factors accounted for 76.3% of the explanatory focus**, versus 34.2% for online-participation characteristics and 18.4% for policymaker characteristics. The paper's central contingency finding: whether an open participation channel actually translates into government responsiveness is determined primarily by institutional characteristics (institutional values/practices, resources, path dependency), not by the quality or design of the participation channel itself. This is a genuine second-order addition to the cluster: F107/F108/F110 show *whether citizens are given voice at all*; F111 shows that *even where voice exists, institutional design — not the channel — determines whether it converts into actual responsiveness*.

### Research-attention imbalance mirrors the governance-level asymmetry

Of the 39 studies, 61.5% examine government-initiated online participation versus only 28.2% citizen-initiated participation (10.3% study both) — the paper's own explicit recommendation is that "future research should focus its efforts on citizen-initiated online participation as this is underrepresented." A meta-level instance of the same asymmetry pattern: even the research infrastructure privileging government-initiated channels over citizen-initiated ones.

### Institutional-explanation sub-taxonomy (Table 4)

Institutional values and practices (36.8% of institutional-explanation articles), institutional resources (26.3%), path dependency and isomorphism (18.4%), political motivations (13.6%) — a directly reusable four-factor breakdown for analysing *which* institutional characteristics determine India's DPI responsiveness specifically, beyond the coarser "institutional factors matter most" headline finding.
