## Summary

James & Quaglia analyse the *emergent regime complexity* generated by 'bigtech' finance — the provision of payment services and financial intermediation by large digital platforms (Amazon, Apple, Google, Alibaba, Meta). The paper argues that bigtech finance sits at the intersection of at least four regulatory regimes (financial, competition, data protection, telecommunications) without a coordinating framework, generating both institutional complexity and *epistemic barriers* — the regulators themselves cannot fully understand the risks because no single regulator has the analytical vantage point required. Extends regime-complex theory (Raustiala & Victor 2004; Alter & Meunier 2009) to the specific case of financial infrastructure disruption by digital platforms.

## Key Findings

### Four-Regime Intersection

| Regime | Governance Focus | Bigtech Finance Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial** | Systemic risk; prudential capital; consumer protection | Are bigtech payment services de facto banking? |
| **Competition** | Market structure; anti-monopoly; interoperability | Does data-network-effect concentration foreclose competition? |
| **Data protection** | Personal data processing; cross-border flows | Can bigtech data pooling be reconciled with GDPR / DPDP? |
| **Telecommunications** | Network infrastructure; universal service | Do bigtech platforms trigger telecom regulatory duties? |

### Epistemic Barriers Diagnosed

James & Quaglia identify three epistemic-barrier mechanisms:
1. **Cross-domain expertise gap** — no regulator has staff with expertise across all four regimes
2. **Data asymmetry** — bigtech firms hold proprietary data that regulators cannot access at scale
3. **Speed asymmetry** — bigtech innovation cycles outpace regulatory rule-making cycles

### Regime-Complex Consequences

The paper argues bigtech finance is a case where regime complexity is *emergent* (not designed) — a byproduct of successive independent regulatory-domain evolution. This distinguishes it from formal regime-complex cases studied in international relations (climate, trade). For governance design, the epistemic barrier is more binding than the coordination barrier: even if regulators coordinated, they would not know what to coordinate on.
