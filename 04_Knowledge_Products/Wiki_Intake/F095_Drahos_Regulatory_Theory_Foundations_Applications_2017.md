## Key Theoretical Frameworks (PRIS-relevant subset)

### Responsive Regulation (Ch. 1, 7) — resolves the "Braithwaite (2017)" citation

The **enforcement pyramid** (Ayres & Braithwaite, 1992, as elaborated here): dialogue/restorative justice at the base, escalating through deterrence to incapacitation/coercion at the peak — the direct theoretical baseline PRIS's fsQCA thesis chapter already cites as the standard "Type I configurations sit outside this" comparator. **Tripartism**: drawing third parties/public-interest groups into the regulatory relationship, moving beyond a pure regulator-regulated dyad. **Types of responsiveness** (Ch. 7) extends this into a "networked escalation pyramid" (Drahos), directly relevant if PRIS ever wants to theorise multi-regulator (RBI/SEBI/CERT-In) escalation dynamics rather than treating each regulator's enforcement ladder in isolation.

### Meta-Regulation (Ch. 9)

"Regulating self-regulation" — originally cost-benefit analysis of regulatory activity (Reagan, 1983), expanded to cover any institution regulating other institutions' regulatory activity. Directly applicable to how NCAIC's proposed coordinating role over RBI/SEBI/MeitY's individual AI governance activities could be theorised.

### Smart Regulation (Ch. 8)

A five-category policy-instrument taxonomy — command-and-control, economic instruments, self-regulation, voluntarism, information strategies — plus the design principle that government's preferred role is creating preconditions for second/third parties to assume regulatory burden. A directly reusable classification scheme for coding India's AI governance instruments (most are currently advisory/information-strategy tier; RBI FREE-AI and SEBI Ch. IIIB are command-and-control tier).

### Nodal Governance (Ch. 10)

Governing nodes: "organisational sites... where attempts are made to intentionally shape the flow of events" (Wood & Shearing, 2007). Four analytical elements: mentalities, technologies, resources, institutions. **This is arguably the most directly transferable construct in the book** — it is the precise theoretical vocabulary for describing India's AI governance landscape as multiple independent "nodes" (RBI, SEBI, MeitY, CERT-In, NCAIC, judiciary) each attempting to shape the same underlying flow of events (AI deployment) without a single governing centre — the theoretical foundation underneath PRIS's own [[Regulatory_Parallelism]] construct.

### Regulatory Capitalism and the Regulatory State (Ch. 16, 17)

Regulatory capitalism: "capitalism is a regulatory institution... constituted, shaped, constrained and expanded as a historically woven patchwork of regulatory institutions" — three regulatory functions (constraining, empowering, constitutive). The regulatory state: governance via rule-promulgation with arm's-length compliance oversight by public agencies — the general theoretical frame explaining *why* RBI/SEBI/CERT-In operate as independent statutory regulators rather than direct ministerial control, relevant background for P4's doctrinal analysis.

### Time and Temporality Tactics (Ch. 18) — candidate fsQCA/RL calibration evidence

Two directly reusable taxonomies: (1) **five lawmaking temporal tactics** — staging, compressing, expanding, segmenting, multiplying time; (2) **six tactics of time-as-resistance by weaker states** — delay, symbolic compliance (partial/perverse implementation, enact-but-subvert-via-regulations, enact-but-fail-to-enforce, enact-but-subvert-courts), fragmenting international regulators, segmenting reforms, invoking cultural incompatibility, substituting an alternative solution. **This second taxonomy is a candidate analytical lens for coding India's regulatory response pattern to international AI-governance pressure** (e.g., principles-based-but-non-binding frameworks as a form of "symbolic compliance") — worth flagging to [[fsQCA_Thesis_Chapter]] as a possible RL (Regulatory Logic) condition-calibration reference.

### Compliance: 14 Questions / Nielsen-Parker Model (Ch. 13)

14 compliance questions derived from the 'Table of Eleven' (T11), split into spontaneous-compliance factors (economic/social/normative motives, capacity) and enforced-compliance factors (respect for regulator, deterrence). A formal compliance-assessment framework, potentially reusable for structuring practitioner-facing compliance diagnostics.

### Procedural Justice (Ch. 3) and Motivational Postures (Ch. 2)

Procedural justice criteria: respect, neutrality, trustworthiness, voice. Five motivational postures toward regulatory authority: commitment, capitulation, resistance, disengagement, game-playing. Relevant vocabulary if PRIS's consulting work (AEGIS_OS, PrivacyWeave) ever needs to characterise regulated entities' compliance posture toward AI governance obligations.

### Legal Pluralism and Transnational Non-State Regulation (Ch. 14, 20)

Legal pluralism: state law as one ordering system among several (customary norms, industry self-regulation) — relevant to India's parallel formal (RBI/SEBI) and informal/soft-law (NITI Aayog advisory) AI governance tracks. Transnational non-state regulation: structural authority (market/investment leverage) vs. discursive authority (framing/meaning-shaping power) — relevant to Big Tech's role (Google, Microsoft, OpenAI) in shaping AI governance norms ahead of binding regulation, a dynamic the corpus's F-series Big Tech intake notes (F070–F085) document empirically without this theoretical framing.

### Public Accountability (Ch. 12)

Five architectural modalities: elections, rationalised bureaucracies, judicial review, transparency, markets — a structured taxonomy for classifying which accountability mechanism(s) any given AI governance instrument relies on.
