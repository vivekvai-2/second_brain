## 3–5 Reusable Findings

1. **AIGov4Gov framework — three-level structure, direct P4 comparator** (feeds S6, P4): A proposed AI governance framework for public organizations operating at strategic (AI strategy/policy/ethics codes), tactical (data governance, risk mitigation, cybersecurity), and operational (AI system development practices) levels, explicitly interacting with government central-agency guidelines. This is structurally close to P4's own **Three-Level Governance Alignment Model** (RBI/SEBI jurisdiction allocation + coordination mechanisms + DPDP integration) — worth a direct structural comparison in P4's literature review or theoretical positioning to differentiate the two (AIGov4Gov is single-organization/public-sector-general; P4's model is multi-regulator/BFSI-specific).

2. **QCA methodology applied directly to AI governance — fsQCA precedent** (feeds fsQCA_Thesis_Chapter, methods): Uses both crisp-set and fuzzy-set QCA on Set Theory/Boolean-operations logic across 28 public organizations (3–250 case range cited as QCA's applicable sample-size band). Calibration anchors: 0.05/0.95 for fuzzy extremes, crossover point at 60 (on a 0–100 survey-scored scale: 100/67/33/0 for "Yes completely"/"Yes partially"/"planned"/"no plan"). Necessary-condition consistency scores reported per proposition (0.83–0.97 range). This is a directly citable, recently-published (Jan 2025) worked example of fsQCA calibration methodology applied to AI governance — useful as a methods-section precedent and citation for [[02_Projects/fsQCA_Thesis_Chapter]].

3. **Strategic-to-operational cascade — strong empirical support** (feeds Institutional_Coherence, Governance_Capacity): Propositions 1A–1D2 test whether data-related, risk-related, cybersecurity, and AI-development practices "must follow guidelines established at the strategic level" — and find strong consistency support throughout (e.g., 0.959862 for data processes, 0.941645 for risk processes, 0.972318 for monitoring/oversight/feedback practices). Concretely: organizations with the strongest strategic-level AI governance actions show correspondingly high implementation of tactical/operational practices — direct empirical evidence for a strategic-cascade governance-capacity model, comparable to this vault's own "sequenced institutional-capacity-building" observation about DPDP Rules 2025's staggered Board-then-obligations commencement ([[07_Institutions/Corpus/MeitY/MeitY_4_DPDP_Rules_2025]]).

4. **Outsourcing/code-access transparency gap** (feeds Governance_Capacity, Accountability, consulting): 73.33% of the sample outsourced at least part of their AI system development, but only 46.43% had access to their own AI system's code — a sharp, quantified governance-capacity gap (organizations formally "governing" AI systems they cannot technically inspect). Average score for transparency-oriented development practices was correspondingly low (50.11/100) and for ethics/bias-representation practices also low (51.11/100), versus a much higher score for automatic monitoring (71.57/100) — suggesting organizations invest more in post-hoc monitoring than in upstream code-level transparency. Directly relevant to any AI vendor-governance/AEGIS_OS-adjacent consulting conversation about outsourced-AI oversight limits.

5. **International comparator leads not yet in corpus** (feeds P1/International comparators): Names several international instruments/initiatives the corpus has not yet ingested — Danish Government (2020) data-ethics law, the Finnish "Aurora" public-private AI knowledge-transfer partnership (2019), and a Nordic Council of Ministers (2018) agreement on AI best-practice implementation across Nordic countries. Also notes the EU AI Act was "in an advanced discussion stage but not yet approved as law at the time of data collection" — a useful dating anchor confirming this study's fieldwork predates the AI Act's 2024 adoption. Candidate leads for future gap-directed sourcing if Nordic/Danish AI governance becomes a research priority.
