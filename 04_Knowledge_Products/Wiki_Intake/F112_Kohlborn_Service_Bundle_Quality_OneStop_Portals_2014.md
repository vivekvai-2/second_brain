## Key Findings and Framework

### Service bundling — core construct

Service bundling for governmental one-stop portals is "the act of aggregating services into a packaged offering, which is aligned with a citizen need" — services grouped around life events ("Buying a house," "Getting married") or by demographics/topic, rather than by the government department that happens to provide them. Directly relevant vocabulary for assessing whether India's DPI-adjacent portals (e.g., UMANG, various state single-window platforms) are organised around citizen life-events or around departmental structure — the "traditional bureaucratic paradigm" (Ho, 2002) this literature explicitly contrasts against.

### Multi-layer quality framework

A four-layer quality model (Halaris, Magoutas, Papadomichelaki & Mentzas, 2007, synthesising 36 prior quality approaches): Behavioural Intentions → Satisfaction → Quality → Process Performance → Technical Performance, cross-cut by Subjective-vs-Objective and Consumer-vs-Provider perspectives, and by three units of analysis (Portal / Bundle / e-Service). A directly reusable structural framework for scoping *what level* a PRIS portal-quality assessment would need to target.

### Practitioner-usable quality dimensions

Four bundle-quality constructs proposed for further validation: **Navigation** (inverse of effort to reach desired information/functionality), **Ease of learning** (inverse of effort to acquire knowledge about the bundle), **Ease of understanding** (inverse of effort to comprehend the bundle's purpose), **Organisation** (degree to which content is perceived as sufficiently organised to be useful). Directly usable as a lightweight practitioner checklist for assessing any citizen-facing DPI portal's usability, distinct from (and complementary to) F110's Munzner-derived visualization-specific design principles.

### Explicit public/private-sector transferability caveat

"Care should be taken when dimensions and constructs, initially intended for assessing quality of the relevant unit of analysis in the private sector, are to be utilised in the public domain, as their applicability might be different" — most of the 26 synthesised e-service quality studies originate in private-sector service-quality research; the paper explicitly flags that public-sector application requires validation, not direct transplantation.
