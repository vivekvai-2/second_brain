## Document Summary

An ELI Innovation Paper proposing 12 Guiding Principles for automated decision-making (ADM) governance in the EU legal order. The paper is a pre-enactment doctrinal contribution written in anticipation of the EU AI Act (then at proposal stage, COM(2021) 206 final). Its central contribution is a principled framework for ADM liability and accountability that addresses the "automation defence" problem — the tendency of operators to deny legal attribution because a decision was made by an automated system. The paper draws from GDPR Art. 22, DSA, DMA, P2B Regulation, and UNCITRAL Model Laws.

## Core Content

### 1. ADM Definition

> "A (computational) process, including AI techniques and approaches, that, fed by inputs and data received or collected from the environment, can generate, given a set of predefined objectives, outputs in a wide variety of forms (content, ratings, recommendations, decisions, predictions, etc.)" (Rodríguez de las Heras Ballell, 2022)

**Two output categories:**

| Category | Type | Effect on Affected Person |
|---|---|---|
| **(2a) Non-decision outputs** | Ratings, rankings, recommendations | Indirect — affected person relies on ADM output for subsequent human or automated decision |
| **(2b) Decision outputs** | Decisions with legal or significant effects on affected person | Direct — ADM output is itself the decision; immediate legal effect |

### 2. Key Parties

| Party | Definition | Legal Mapping |
|---|---|---|
| **Operator** | Entity that deploys and benefits from ADM (control-and-benefit binomial) | Maps to "user" in AI Act; analogous to GDPR "data controller" |
| **Affected Person** | Natural or legal person subject to the effects of an ADM output | Maps to "data subject" in GDPR; "affected person" in AI Act FRIA |

### 3. Twelve Guiding Principles

| # | Principle | Core Obligation | Legislative Anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Law-compliant ADM | Operator must ensure ADM complies with all law applicable to an equivalent non-automated system | General EU law |
| **2** | Non-discrimination against ADM | ADM outputs shall not be denied legal effect solely because they are automated | UNCITRAL Model Laws on Electronic Commerce/Government |
| **3** | Attribution of decisions to operator | Operator cannot deny attribution of an ADM decision solely on grounds that it was automated | Principle resolving "automation defence" |
| **4** | Disclosure of automated nature | Affected person must be informed that a decision is automated (unless obvious or unnecessary) | GDPR Art. 22; DSA Arts. 15, 17, 20, 22 |
| **5** | Traceable decisions | ADM shall enable traceability of any decision taken (logging by design); traceability ≠ full transparency | GDPR; AI Act (proposed) |
| **6** | Reasoned decisions | Complexity or opacity of ADM is not a valid ground for unreasoned or arbitrary decisions; affected persons have a right to reasons | GDPR Art. 22; Directive on Platform Work Art. 8 |
| **7** | Allocation of risks to operator | Operator bears risks arising from ADM based on control-and-benefit test | General liability law; P2B Regulation |
| **8** | No limitations to rights and access to justice | Affected persons retain rights; alternative human-based route to justice must be available | EU Charter of Fundamental Rights Art. 47 |
| **9** | Human oversight and action | Reasonable and proportionate human oversight of ADM; not necessarily continuous HITL | AI Act (proposed); GDPR Art. 22 |
| **10** | Human review of significant decisions | Upon request of affected person, significant ADM decisions must be subject to human review | GDPR Art. 22(3); Directive on Platform Work Art. 8 |
| **11** | Responsible ADM | Operators must be aware of ADM's socio-economic impact on democratic values, fundamental rights, dignity, social cohesion | EU Charter; UNESCO AI Ethics |
| **12** | Risk-based approach | All principles applied proportionately to the nature, scale, and risk level of the ADM system | Proportionality; AI Act risk tiers |

### 4. The "Control-and-Benefit" Operator Standard

The paper's most significant doctrinal contribution. The operator is identified through a binomial test:
- **Control**: Does the entity control the design, deployment, and operational parameters of the ADM system?
- **Benefit**: Does the entity derive commercial, operational, or decision-making benefit from the ADM output?

Both prongs must be satisfied for "operator" attribution. This mirrors GDPR's "data controller" definition and predicts the EU AI Act's "deployer" concept.

### 5. Key Legislative References

**Operative:** GDPR Art. 22 (ADM centerpiece); DSA Arts. 15/17/20/22; DMA; P2B Regulation; Directive on Platform Work; EU Charter of Fundamental Rights; UNCITRAL Model Laws on Electronic Commerce and Electronic Government

**Anticipatory (pre-enactment):** AI Act Commission Proposal COM(2021) 206 final; EP Resolution on AI civil liability (2020/2014(INL)); EU Expert Group on Liability and New Technologies Report (2019)

## Analytical Notes

**Resolves the automation defence.** Principle 3 (Attribution) is the paper's most original doctrinal contribution. It directly counters the "automation defence" — where operators disclaim liability for AI-generated decisions on the grounds that the system, not the operator, decided. This is the core accountability gap in ADM law, and ELI's resolution (control-and-benefit → operator attribution) is the most developed pre-AI Act formulation available.

**VERIFY critical: "proposed AI Act" is now enacted.** The paper was written in 2022. All references to the "proposed Artificial Intelligence Act" must be read against the actual enacted AI Act (entered into force August 2024; obligations applying 2025–2026). The ELI Principles generally align with the enacted Act, but specific cross-references need updating before citation in current-date academic work.

**Principles 9 and 10 → EU AI Act HITL.** The paper's Principles 9 (Human Oversight) and 10 (Human Review) anticipate and align with the EU AI Act's human oversight obligation (Art. 14) for high-risk AI systems. The distinction between continuous oversight (Principle 9 = proportionate) and on-request review (Principle 10 = mandatory) maps cleanly onto the Act's operational requirements.

**Traceability ≠ transparency (Principle 5).** The paper makes a technically important distinction: traceability (logging by design; auditable record) is a necessary but not sufficient condition for transparency (human-intelligible explanation). This distinction has direct implications for AI Act explainability obligations and for F041's Transparent Methods requirement.

**Doctrinal value for P4 (IJLIT).** The ELI framework provides the most developed EU legal doctrine for ADM governance prior to the AI Act. Combined with F039 (ICAAD rights-harm mapping), the two documents form a complete doctrinal foundation for a comparative India-EU ADM governance analysis. India's DPDP Act (2023) lacks an Art. 22–equivalent ADM restriction — this is a significant governance gap for P4.

**Governance_Debt signal.** Principle 12's risk-based approach was a recommendation in 2022; the EU AI Act enacted it in 2024. Two-year lag between scholarly recommendation and regulatory enactment. For jurisdictions outside the EU (including India), the lag is longer still — evidencing ongoing [[Governance_Debt]].
