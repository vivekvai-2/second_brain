## Document Summary

A cross-institutional research report mapping AI harm against ten fundamental rights drawn from **8** international human rights instruments *(corrected 2026-07-15 — see note below; original intake stated 9)*. The document synthesises the emerging legal and institutional framework for AI harm accountability, including the Council of Europe AI Convention (CETS 225), the EU AI Act's fundamental rights impact assessment obligation (Art. 27), and the UN Human Rights Council Working Group findings (May 2025). The primary contribution is a structured rights-harm mapping (Table 2) and a harm taxonomy synthesis across five major AI risk classification frameworks.

### 1. Rights-Harm Mapping (Table 2)

The document maps AI harm across 10 fundamental rights, sourced from **8** international instruments (corrected 2026-07-15):

**Human rights instruments referenced:**
UDHR, CERD (Racial Discrimination), ICCPR, ICESCR, CEDAW (Women), CRC (Children), CMW (Migrant Workers), CRPD (Persons with Disabilities) — **8 instruments** *(corrected 2026-07-15: original intake listed 9, incorrectly appending SDGs — a development-goals framework, not a human rights instrument. NotebookLM re-extraction of the document's own Table 1 confirms 8 instruments, no SDGs. This correction should be read wherever "9 instruments" appears elsewhere in the vault.)*

**Ten fundamental rights mapped:**

| Right | AI Harm Vectors |
|---|---|
| **Equality and non-discrimination** | Biased training data; proxy discrimination; automated screening in employment, credit, criminal justice |
| **Privacy** | Surveillance, facial recognition, inferential profiling, data aggregation harms |
| **Procedural fairness** | ADM without explanation; denial of human review; lack of contestability |
| **Freedom from physical/psychological harm** | AI-enabled violence, autonomous weapons, psychologically manipulative systems |
| **Freedom of expression and information** | Algorithmic content suppression, disinformation amplification, surveillance chilling effects |
| **Right to meaningful employment** | Labour displacement, deskilling, AI-mediated algorithmic management |
| **Right to benefit from scientific progress** | Unequal access to AI benefits; exclusion of Global South populations |
| **Prohibition of hate advocacy** | AI-generated hate speech at scale; automated incitement amplification |
| **Freedom of assembly and association** | Surveillance and prediction of collective action; chilling of political association |
| **Right to participate in public affairs** | Disinformation targeting democratic processes; exclusion from AI governance |

### 5. Key Accountability Claim

> "AI that cannot comply with international human rights law must be banned or suspended." — Volker Türk, UN High Commissioner for Human Rights (quoted in document)

This is the strongest direct accountability claim in the rights corpus — it frames international human rights law as a *floor* for AI system legality, not merely an aspirational standard.

**Fuller Türk quote (added 2026-07-15, NotebookLM re-extraction — original intake used the compressed accountability soundbite only):**
> "The starting point should be the harms that people experience and will likely experience. This requires listening to those who are affected... and any AI regulations need to require assessment of the human rights risks and impacts of AI systems before, during, and after their use... AI technologies that cannot be operated in compliance with international human rights law must be banned or suspended until such adequate safeguards are in place." — Volker Türk, 2023

### 6. Key Quotes *(added 2026-07-15, NotebookLM re-extraction — not captured at original intake)*

- **CoE Explanatory Report** (to the Framework Convention): "...artificial intelligence systems offer unprecedented opportunities to protect and promote human rights, democracy and the rule of law. At the same time, they also wished to acknowledge that there are serious risks and perils arising from certain activities within the lifecycle of artificial intelligence such as, for instance, discrimination in a variety of contexts, gender inequality, the undermining of democratic processes, impairing human dignity or individual autonomy, or the misuses of artificial intelligence systems by some States for repressive purposes, in violation of international human rights law."
- **EU AI Act Explanatory Memorandum**: "...opacity, complexity, bias, a certain degree of unpredictability and partially autonomous behaviour of certain AI systems..."
- **OECD AI Principles**: "AI actors should respect the rule of law, human rights, democratic and human-centred values throughout the AI system lifecycle. These include nondiscrimination and equality, freedom, dignity, autonomy of individuals, privacy and data protection, diversity, fairness, social justice, and internationally recognised labour rights."
- **2023 Bletchley Declaration**: "...[the] potential for serious, even catastrophic, harm, either deliberate or unintentional, stemming from the most significant capabilities of these AI models."
- **UN Working Group report (May 2025)**: "...although the protection of human rights is increasingly emphasized in regulatory developments...evidence from the Working Group's consultations and submissions has shown that there are still significant gaps when it comes to legislative frameworks on rights-respecting procurement and deployment of AI systems by States and businesses. Further, for many businesses, understanding of the human rights implications of the deployment of AI systems remains in its early stages. Thus, the rapid, mainly unregulated, uptake of AI systems by States and businesses is creating situations with high potential for adverse impacts across a variety of human rights, in a context where existing access-to-remedy mechanisms are struggling to keep up."
- Named commentator cited but not previously logged: **Kate Jones**, Associate Fellow, Chatham House (2023).

## Analytical Notes

**Rights-based framing as P4 doctrinal layer.** The document provides the most structured legal mapping of AI harm to international human rights instruments available in the Batch 13 cluster. For P4 (IJLIT doctrinal paper), the ICAAD-KWM table provides a ready-made doctrinal framework for situating Indian AI governance obligations (DPDP Act, MeitY framework) within international human rights law.

**⚠ SUPERSEDED (2026-07-31, #226 resolved) — original framing below is now confirmed stale, retained struck-through for audit trail only:**

~~FRIA obligation (EU AI Act Art. 27) from 2 August 2026. The document's reference to the fundamental rights impact assessment obligation — effective from 2 August 2026 — creates an imminent regulatory deadline that is analytically significant. As of this session (June 2026), this is six weeks away. This is the strongest pending implementation hook in the EU AI Act timeline.~~

**Corrected framing:** the Digital Omnibus on AI was formally published in the Official Journal and took effect **27 July 2026**. Article 27 itself was not textually amended, but its practical application is tied to the Annex III high-risk classification trigger date, which the Digital Omnibus deferred from 2 August 2026 to **2 December 2027** (2 August 2028 for Annex I product-embedded AI). **The FRIA obligation is therefore no longer "six weeks away" — it is now approximately 16 months away (2 December 2027)**, confirmed via Perplexity Deep Research against the finalised Digital Omnibus text — see [[07_Institutions/Corpus/International/International_22_EU_AI_Act_Conformity_Assessment_Notified_Bodies]]'s VERIFY #226 resolution for full sourcing. **Do not cite the original "six weeks away" framing above in any publication-track material** — it reflects the pre-Digital-Omnibus regulatory timeline and is now materially incorrect.

**Türk quote as accountability anchor.** The UN High Commissioner's quote frames human rights law as a binding constraint, not a soft norm. This framing supports the [[Accountability]] claim that AI system operators (deployers under EU AI Act; "users" under AI Act terminology) bear non-delegable accountability for rights compliance.

**Cross-link to International_17 (CoE AI Convention) and International_18 (UNESCO).** F039 cites CoE AI Convention (CETS 225) and UNESCO AI Ethics Recommendation as the two most relevant international normative anchors. Both are already in the vault — strong intra-vault cross-referencing opportunity.

**HUDERIA methodology.** HUDERIA is an emerging human rights due diligence methodology specifically for AI systems; referenced but not deeply elaborated in the document. Worth tracking as a potential future corpus node.

**40% employment exposure (IMF) caveat.** The IMF 40% figure is widely cited but methodologically contested (exposure ≠ displacement). Use with framing caveat.
