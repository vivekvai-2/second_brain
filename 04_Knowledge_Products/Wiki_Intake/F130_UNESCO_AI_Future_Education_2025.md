## Document Summary

A UNESCO-commissioned anthology of 35 academic contributors examining AI integration in education across philosophical, pedagogical, governance, and equity dimensions. Organised into seven thematic sections: philosophical provocations; powers and perils; pedagogy and assessment; teacher roles; ethical and governance imperatives; coded inequalities; and policy and geopolitical realities. The governance-relevant core is concentrated in Sections 6–8 (Porayska-Pomsta/Asare; Gulson/Sellar; Siemens; Tuomi). Central governance argument: AI in education is not a neutral tool but a political act requiring explicit accountability architectures, participatory design, and counter-governance capacity.

### 4. Governance Taxonomy — Gulson & Sellar (Most PRIS-Relevant Chapter)

Three governance postures toward AI in education:
1. **Accept synthetic governance** — treat AI-human decision amalgamation as inevitable; manage within it
2. **Reform governance** — push for more democratic/ethical design within existing AI governance architectures
3. **Counter-governance** — refuse the frame; reorient toward power redistribution rather than design improvement

## Analytical Notes

**Synthetic governance as Governance_Debt vector.** Gulson & Sellar's concept — where non-human rationalities embedded in algorithms become co-producers of governance decisions — maps onto [[Governance_Debt]]. The Alaska hallucination incident is a concrete worked example: absence of human authorship accountability in a GenAI-assisted policy document is not a one-off error but a systemic gap that accumulates when no pre-deployment governance architecture exists. Unlike the corpus's infrastructure-based Governance_Debt instances (F129's Mumbai cable concentration), this is a *process* variant of governance debt — algorithmic rationality substituting for deliberative governance capacity without any institutional gate.

**NSW AI Assurance Framework as pre-deployment accountability comparator.** The NSW mandatory audit gate (all AI tools must pass before school deployment, with a documented rejection on gender-bias grounds in 2024) is the most concrete education-sector pre-deployment accountability architecture in the corpus. The structural distinction from SEBI_14 and RBI_7 (ex-post sole-responsibility) is analytically significant for P4 doctrinal analysis: one accountability design prevents harm at entry; the other assigns consequence after it occurs. This distinction — gate accountability vs. attribution accountability — is not currently theorised in the corpus.

**Digital inequality as Governance_Capacity gap.** The 90%/35%/26% figures extend the [[Governance_Capacity]] deficit narrative from physical infrastructure (F129's subsea cables) to human capital. A state cannot govern AI effectively if its population cannot access it or participate in producing it. These figures are also relevant to the fsQCA Stakeholder Inclusion (SI) condition calibration: a jurisdiction with 90% of adolescent girls offline cannot claim high SI scores regardless of formal consultation architecture.

**Peripheral positioning for PRIS.** Education-sector AI governance is not a primary domain for P1–P4. The PRIS relevance is concept-level (synthetic governance and counter-governance vocabulary; NSW comparator for accountability gate design; digital inequality quantitative evidence) rather than instrument-level. This node should be cited for comparative worked examples and conceptual vocabulary enrichment, not as a primary regulatory instrument.
