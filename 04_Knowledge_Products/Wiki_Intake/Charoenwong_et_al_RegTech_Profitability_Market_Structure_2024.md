## Summary

Charoenwong et al. conduct the corpus's most rigorous empirical study of RegTech — compliance-driven investment in technology — and its consequences for firm profitability, operations, and market structure in the financial sector. The study exploits a natural experiment (new internal control requirements) to identify causal effects on affected firms. Key finding: firms make significant ERP and hardware investments in response to compliance mandates; these expenditures enable complementary investments that are then leveraged for non-compliance purposes. IT budgets rise, profits fall (especially at small firms), and acquisition activity + market concentration increase. The paper is the corpus's foundational empirical demonstration that regulation-driven RegTech investment reshapes financial-sector market structure — with important implications for [[Regulatory_Parallelism]] cost accumulation and for the SME segment (F064 Trierweiler & Krumay).

## Key Findings and Framework

### Empirical Design

- **Setting:** New internal control requirements imposed on financial-sector firms (⚠VERIFY specific regulatory event)
- **Identification:** Difference-in-differences design comparing affected vs unaffected firms
- **Outcome variables:** IT spending, profits, customer complaints, misconduct, market share, acquisitions

### Central Empirical Findings

| Outcome | Direction | Magnitude / Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| ERP + hardware investment | ↑ (significant) | Compliance mandates drive substantial IT expenditure |
| IT budgets | ↑ | Persistent budget increase, not one-off |
| Profits | ↓ (especially small firms) | Compliance cost accumulates disproportionately at SMEs |
| Customer complaints | ↓ (modest) | Some complementary benefit from compliance-driven IT |
| Misconduct | ↓ (modest) | Some deterrent effect from monitoring |
| Market concentration | ↑ | Small firms exit or acquired; market consolidates |
| Acquisitions | ↑ | Consolidation channel confirmed |

### Central Argument

Compliance-driven RegTech investment produces the *appearance* of market efficiency gains (reduced customer complaints, reduced misconduct) but the *distributional* impact is asymmetric — small firms bear disproportionate cost, leading to consolidation. RegTech investment mandates thus have a hidden market-structure consequence beyond their stated compliance-quality objective.
