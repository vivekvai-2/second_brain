## Key Findings and Framework

### Executive oversight distinctiveness for autonomous AI systems (Episode 17, "Strategic Directions for AI")

Direct governance claim: AI strategy requires distinctive governance mechanisms because systems make decisions autonomously, so "executive oversight must be qualitatively different from the oversight applied to passive IT systems." This is an academic articulation of the same structural argument underlying [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Standard/Agentic_AI_Governance]]'s "Five Structural Breaks" (decision velocity eliminating the human review window), reached independently from a management-theory rather than a runtime-enforcement angle. Paired with **AI Strategic Orientations** (Li et al., 2021): exploitation (optimising existing processes), exploration (discovering new capabilities), and ambidexterity (pursuing both) — a strategic-choice taxonomy for how enterprises deploy AI, adaptable as a governance-maturity lens.

### Three-Layer Accountability Model (Episode 24, "Are Designers of Digital Technologies Responsible for Them?")

Martin (2019)'s model distinguishes technical designers, deployers, and affected parties as three distinct accountability layers for AI systems — a named, citable academic accountability architecture, structurally comparable to the corpus's existing maker-checker and RACI-based instances (CERT_In_1, NCAIC_1, F079) but derived from IS/ethics theory rather than a regulatory or consultancy source.

### AI fairness as socio-technical redesign, not algorithmic constraint (Episode 20, "Can AI be Fair?")

Central claim: "fairness in AI systems cannot be achieved by technical constraints on algorithms alone — it requires redesigning the socio-technical system within which AI and humans interact." Distinguishes procedural fairness (how a decision is made) from distributive fairness (what outcomes result), drawing on Socio-Technical Systems Theory (Trist & Bamforth, 1951). A useful theoretical counterweight to purely technical fairness-metric framings elsewhere in the corpus.

### AI ground truth as an organisational and social product, not a neutral input (Episode 21, "Is AI Ground Truth Really 'True'?")

From ethnographic work on medical-imaging AI (Lebovitz, Lifshitz-Assaf & Levina, NYU Stern): recommends treating training-data labels as socially constructed by the experts who produce them, not as neutral ground truth — "interview and observe the experts who produce ground truth labels; treat training data as an organisational and social product." Directly relevant to any future PRIS work on AI data-quality or training-data governance obligations (cf. CERT_In SBOM/AIBOM series).

### Professional jurisdiction and human-AI coordination (Episode 15, "When the Machine Meets the Expert")

Applies Professional Jurisdiction Theory (Abbott, 1988) to how domain experts progressively modify AI systems to preserve their professional authority — a lens on human-AI coordination distinct from pure automation-substitution framing, relevant to workforce-impact governance questions.

### PAPA Framework — foundational IS ethics taxonomy (referenced across the AI mini-series)

Mason (1986)'s four ethical issues of the information age — Privacy, Accuracy, Property, Accessibility — is named as a foundational, pre-AI-era IS ethics taxonomy still applied to frame AI ethics discussions. Useful as a citable historical anchor predating the corpus's AI-specific ethics frameworks (e.g., Jobin et al. 2019, F094) by three decades.

### Contribution-framing discipline reinforced (Episode 22, "Theorizing about New Technology? No Problem!")

Restates and sharpens the violated-assumption contribution test already captured in F097: "the first two paragraphs of any IS theory paper must establish not just a puzzle but a violation of a belief the reader holds." A four-step method is given: identify the phenomenon firsthand; identify the assumption in existing theory it challenges; collect data illustrating the violation; propose theoretical extension. This is the same craft discipline F097 flagged as relevant to the GIQ-rejection lesson — no new VERIFY or concept action required here, noted for continuity only.

### Career-strategy additions (Episode 25, "The IS Field Has No Passion" — Kalle Lyytinen)

Extends F097's career-advice cluster: attend doctoral consortia even without presenting; use conference presentations to pitch a research agenda; treat job talks as performances of scholarly identity; cold-email senior scholars proposing a 15-minute conversation. Lyytinen's specific advice — "do not let the Basket of 8 list determine what problems you investigate" — is a direct counterpoint worth weighing against [[01_Strategy/VV_Publication_Pipeline_v3]]'s AP-02 (optimise for earliest acceptance probability), noted as a tension rather than resolved.
