## Key Findings and Framework

### The Pillars framework — regulative, normative, cultural-cognitive elements

Scott's central, most widely cited construct: institutions are "comprised of cultural-cognitive, normative and regulative elements that, together with associated activities and resources, provide stability and meaning to social life" (Scott 2001: 48). Different theoretical traditions emphasise different pillars — economists and rational-choice theorists stress **regulative** elements (rules, sanctions); early sociologists stress **normative** elements (values, norms, professional standards); organisational sociologists and cultural anthropologists stress **cultural-cognitive** elements (shared frames, taken-for-granted scripts). Directly transferable to PRIS: Institutional_Coherence's own four dimensions (cross-referencing, jurisdictional clarity, obligation consistency, coordination mechanisms) sit closest to Scott's regulative pillar; the construct's "form vs. substance" distinction (documented empirically across F094, F103, F121) can be read as a regulative/cultural-cognitive gap — instruments converge at the regulative (rule-label) level while diverging at the cultural-cognitive (shared-meaning, operational-interpretation) level.

### Rationalized myths and decoupling — Meyer & Rowan lineage

Scott traces the "bolder conception" of institutional theory to Meyer & Rowan's (1977) argument that organisations adopt "rationalized myths" — rule-like systems whose efficacy depends on being "widely shared, or... promulgated by individuals or groups that have been granted the right to determine such matters." Formal structures that "celebrate institutionalized myths" can differ from structures that act efficiently — the origin of the **decoupling** concept (formal compliance structure vs. actual operational practice), later "conditionalized" by Tolbert & Zucker's (1996) qualifier: "to be institutional, structure must generate action." This is the deepest theoretical root of the coherence-in-form/incoherence-in-substance pattern PRIS has independently documented empirically (F094, F103, F121) — decoupling theory supplies the general causal mechanism (institutionalised myths adopted for legitimacy, not efficiency) for why that empirical pattern should be expected.

### Institutional pressures — coercive, normative, mimetic (DiMaggio & Powell)

The "East-Coast variant": DiMaggio & Powell's (1983) three isomorphism mechanisms — **coercive** pressure (from institutional agents, e.g. regulators), **normative** pressure (from professional standards), and **mimetic** pressure (imitation of similar/related organisations, especially under uncertainty). Directly applicable vocabulary for classifying *why* PRIS's Regulatory_Parallelism instances converge without coordination: several documented parallelism cases (e.g., F119's narrative-level isomorphism across 33 countries; F121's meta-level convergence across four independent research teams) are better described as mimetic isomorphism (independent actors converging under uncertainty by imitating perceived legitimate models) than as coercive or normative convergence.

### Strategic responses to institutional demands (Oliver 1991)

A six-category taxonomy of how organisations respond to institutional pressure, ranging from full compliance to active resistance: **acquiesce, compromise, avoidance, defiance, and manipulation**. A directly reusable typology for characterising how regulated BFSI entities in the PRIS corpus respond to India's fragmented, parallel AI-governance mandates — most of the corpus's evidence documents entities acquiescing to parallel obligations (absorbing duplicated compliance cost) rather than the more active compromise/avoidance/defiance/manipulation responses Oliver's taxonomy also anticipates.

### Levels of analysis — organisational field as the PRIS-relevant unit

A five-level analytical hierarchy: (1) individual/group within an organisation, (2) the organisation itself, (3) the organisational set, (4) the organisation population, (5) the **organisation field** — "an interdependent collection of similar and dissimilar organisations operating in the same domain." India's AI-governance landscape (RBI, SEBI, MeitY, CERT-In, IRDAI, TRAI, ICMR, NCAIC, judiciary) is precisely an organisational field in Scott's sense — a directly citable theoretical anchor for describing the unit of analysis Institutional_Coherence and Regulatory_Parallelism operate at, distinguishing it explicitly from organisation-level or population-level analysis.

### Technical vs. institutional pressures vary by sector (Scott & Meyer 1983)

A worked sectoral taxonomy: educational organisations face stronger institutional than technical pressures; industrial concerns the reverse; banks and nuclear power plants face strong pressures of both types; U.S. child-care centres, weak pressures of both types. Directly relevant to characterising India's BFSI sector (banks/financial regulators) as a "strong pressures of both types" case in Scott's own typology — a citable theoretical justification for why BFSI AI governance is simultaneously technically demanding (model risk, operational resilience) and institutionally demanding (regulatory legitimacy, coordination expectations), rather than one or the other.
