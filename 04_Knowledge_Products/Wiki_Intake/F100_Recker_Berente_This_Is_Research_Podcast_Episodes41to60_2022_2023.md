## Key Findings and Framework

### "That's Interesting" test — the citation resolved (Episode 43, "Writing Papers on How to Write Papers")

Davis, M. (1971), *That's Interesting! Towards a Phenomenology of Sociology and a Sociology of Phenomenology* — named explicitly here as the foundational test of theoretical contribution (does the paper challenge an assumption the audience already holds). **This resolves an inference F097 made without a full citation** ("the podcast's reference list points to Murray Davis's 'That's Interesting' tradition") — now confirmed as Davis (1971), directly citable. Paired with Berente's unpublished "nine-sentence introduction" scaffold (phenomenon, theory gap, research question, method, finding, contribution) as a concrete pre-drafting tool.

### Editorial-dysfunction literacy (Episodes 41, 51)

Advisory: "select journals based on editorial track records, use forums like the AISWorld listserv to gauge editorial cultures, and do not internalize rejection as a reflection of research quality when editorial dysfunction may be the cause." Sarker et al. (2015)'s framework is named as the normative backdrop against which dysfunctional Senior Editor behaviour is measured. Directly relevant given the GIQ desk-rejection experience already logged in [[01_Strategy/VV_Publication_Pipeline_v3]] — a reminder to weigh editorial-culture fit alongside manuscript quality when re-targeting.

### Four Golden Rules for co-authorship (Episode 49) — extends F099's Berente Rules

"(1) agree upfront on authorship order using merit-based criteria; (2) establish clear milestones and deadlines before beginning the project; (3) communicate expectations about revision workloads explicitly; (4) never change authorship order unilaterally without full team consensus." A later, complementary set to F099's "Berente Rules for Starting Collaborations" — together the two episodes form a fuller co-authorship governance protocol (start-of-project rules + in-project rules).

### Data privacy and governance theory cluster (Episode 44, "Talking Data Privacy and Governance") — highest-value secondary finding

Kirsten Martin's critique: the "notice and choice" model (privacy notices + consent) is "fundamentally broken" as a governance mechanism. Three alternative theoretical framings are named: **Privacy as Trust** (Waldman, 2018 — privacy as a relational norm, not a transactional commodity), the **Relational Theory of Data Governance** (Viljoen, 2021 — a structural account moving beyond individual consent), and the **CARE Theory of Dignity** (Leidner & Tona, 2021 — an IS-specific personal-data-ethics framework). **Potentially significant for PRIS:** India's DPDP Act 2025/Rules architecture is itself substantially consent-manager/notice-based — this academic critique of notice-and-choice as structurally inadequate is a citable theoretical counterpoint worth holding in reserve for any future P1/thought-leadership content critiquing DPDP's consent-centric design, though **not asserted here as a finding about DPDP itself** — this is IS privacy theory, not a claim about any specific instrument, and no DPDP corpus node has been edited on this basis.

### Causal-methods taxonomy corroboration (Episode 46, "Causality Meets Diversity")

Potential outcomes framework (Neyman-Rubin), structural causal models/do-calculus (Pearl 2009), quasi-experimental methods (difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity), Granger causality, and fsQCA are named together as the field's causal-identification toolkit. Corroborates but adds no new technical detail to [[08_Methods/fsQCA]]'s existing anchor set — no edit made, consistent with F097/F099's established practice.
