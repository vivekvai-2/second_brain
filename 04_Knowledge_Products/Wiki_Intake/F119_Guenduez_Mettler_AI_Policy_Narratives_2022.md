## Key Findings and Framework

### Four government roles in AI (Fig. 1) — directly reusable classification framework

**Regulator, Enabler, Leader, User** — a clean, four-category taxonomy for classifying any government's stance toward AI. Directly applicable retroactively to classify the corpus's existing India-focused nodes: NITI_7 (2018 opportunity-framing) and MeitY_2 (guidelines) skew toward **Enabler**; RBI FREE-AI and SEBI_14 skew toward **Regulator**; IndiaAI Mission investment activity skews toward **Leader**; government AI-adoption-in-public-administration initiatives (cf. F118) would be classified as **User**. A directly usable analytical lens for the AI Governance Wiki's S1/S3 sections.

### Six policy narratives (Table 1) — with explicit role mapping

1. **Building an AI marketplace** (characters: businesses/startups; moral: government action plans to strengthen economic competitiveness; role: **Enabler**).
2. **Counteracting the winner-takes-all practice** (characters: large companies and state actors; moral: data-sharing with the public sector for reuse and public good; role: **Regulator**) — quotes include France's "to avoid value being vacuumed off by a private actor in a paramount position," Serbia's "obligation to provide the data to state authorities without reimbursement," and Norway's "data sharing may be imposed if necessary... for reasons of public interest." A directly relevant cross-national data-sovereignty-assertion pattern, analytically adjacent to India's own data-localisation debates (DPDP Act, RBI data-localisation mandates).
3. **Engaging in strategic collaboration for AI R&D** (characters: industry/academia/government; role: **Leader**).
4. **Creating ethical and trustworthy AI** (characters: individuals/communities/AI designers/operators; moral: focus on human values and fundamental rights; role: **Regulator**) — cites Singapore's 2019 Model AI Governance Framework (Asia's first) and Malta's explicit human-accountability requirement ("clear processes should be in place to ensure there is always a human who can be held accountable for the operation of an AI system").
5. **Educating AI professionals** (characters: domestic job market/educational institutions/students; role: **Enabler**).
6. **Advancing the deployment of AI in practice** (characters: IT companies/research institutes/industries/government; role: **Enabler**).

**Distribution finding:** four of six narratives map to the Enabler role — national AI policy narratives across 33 countries skew heavily toward economic/capability-building framing over regulatory/rights-protective framing, a citable, quantified data point for characterising the global AI-policy landscape's overall normative centre of gravity as of 2017–2020 (pre-dating the post-ChatGPT regulatory-hardening wave the corpus's later nodes document, e.g. F106's EU AI Act evolution, International_21/22).

### Narrative-level isomorphism — new Regulatory_Parallelism evidence variant

The paper's own headline contribution (5.1.1: "Unveiling the isomorphism in narratives in AI policies") finds that 33 independently-authored national AI policies converge on the same six narrative structures despite no coordination mechanism between them — institutional isomorphism operating at the **rhetorical/narrative level**, distinct from the corpus's existing convergence evidence at the instrument level (most India/BFSI nodes), the principle level (F094's five-principle convergence across 84 ethics-guideline issuers), or the intra-jurisdictional-layering level (F106/International_21/22). A genuinely new parallelism variant: convergence in *how AI policy is narrated and justified*, not merely in *what obligations it imposes*.

### Foundational case reference

*State v. Loomis* (Wisconsin, cited in Background) — the COMPAS algorithmic-sentencing case, invoked as demonstrating how "outsourcing public decision-making to machines could undermine values fundamental to a democracy." A citable, widely-known incident anchor for teaching/consulting content on ADM accountability risk, complementing [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/Sejwal_Gupta_ADM_Indian_Credit_Scoring_2026]] and [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F118_Madan_Ashok_AI_Adoption_Diffusion_Public_Administration_2022|F118]]'s algorithmic-bureaucracy framing.

### Quantified AI-investment comparators

EU: at least €20 billion in AI financial support/uptake encouragement by end-2020 (European Commission, 2018); US: $1.2 billion federal funding for AI-based research/training programmes. Directly comparable investment-scale data points for positioning India's IndiaAI Mission funding internationally.
