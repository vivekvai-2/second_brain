## Key Findings and Framework

### Regulation *of* IT vs. regulation *through* IT — the paper's foundational distinction

The paper's organising conceptual move, made explicit in the title itself: **regulation of IT** (rules governing IT artefacts, infrastructures, algorithms, platforms as regulatory *objects*) is analytically distinct from **regulation through IT** (IT artefacts — GRC systems, RegTech, SupTech — as regulatory *instruments/mechanisms*). This dual framing is directly reusable for classifying the PRIS corpus's own evidence: most of the corpus documents "regulation of AI" (RBI/SEBI/MeitY instruments governing AI systems); a smaller, distinct subset documents "regulation through AI/IT" (audit infrastructure, CERT-In's technical standards, RegTech/SupTech tooling) — a classification axis not previously named explicitly in PRIS's own vocabulary.

### Rules vs. Principles — Ford (2008), directly applicable to PRIS's binding/aspirational coding

A precise, citable distinction: a **rule** "generally entails an advance determination of what conduct is permissible, leaving only factual issues to be determined by the frontline regulator"; a **principle** "may entail leaving both specification of what conduct is permissible and factual issues to the frontline regulator" (Ford 2008: 6–7, Table 2). This is a sharper theoretical grounding for the deontic-strength distinction PRIS already codes informally throughout the corpus (RBI FREE-AI's "should" vs. SEBI_14's "must"/binding statutory language) — directly relevant to [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Signature/Deontic_Bifurcation]] and to Institutional Grammar 2.0 (ADICO) coding practice, supplying a named regulatory-theory citation (Ford 2008, distinct from and complementary to Baldwin/Cave/Lodge already cited in [[05_Concepts/05_Concepts_Standard/Accountability]]'s theoretical positioning) for why rule/principle deontic strength matters analytically, not just descriptively.

### Risk conceptualisation — formal equations with temporal dimensions

Risk is defined per Aven et al. (2018) as "the consequence of an activity and associated uncertainty," formalised as Risk = (C,U)ατ,η — consequence and uncertainty parameterised by activity (α), time (τ), and a further parameter (η). A second equation (R'α1+α2) extends this to **combined/aggregate risk across multiple simultaneous activities** — directly relevant to any PRIS argument about how Regulatory_Parallelism's duplicated obligations translate into aggregate (not merely additive) enterprise risk exposure, a formalisation the corpus's existing Governance_Debt construct currently handles qualitatively rather than with an explicit risk-aggregation model.

### Table 3 — risk-level taxonomy across six innovative IT artefact categories

A structured taxonomy (pp. 94–95) classifying regulatory risk levels across AI, Blockchain/DLT, Smart Contracts, Quantum computing, IoT, and Cloud — a directly reusable cross-technology comparative frame. Useful for situating PRIS's AI-specific corpus evidence within a broader innovative-technology regulatory-risk landscape, and for any future consulting angle extending the existing diagnostic templates beyond AI specifically (the vault's [[Agentic_AI_Security_Diagnostic_Template]] and [[Governance_Diagnostic_Report_Template]] are both AI-scoped; this taxonomy shows the same diagnostic logic is extensible to blockchain/IoT/cloud engagements).

### RegTech and SupTech — precise, citable operational definitions

**RegTech**: "(a) helps firms manage regulatory requirements... (b) enables compliant business systems and data; (c) helps control and manage regulatory, financial and non-financial risks; (d) performs regulatory compliance reporting" (Butler and O'Brien, 2019). **SupTech** performs two activities: (1) Data Collection (reporting + management) and (2) Data Analytics (market surveillance / misconduct analysis / micro-prudential / macro-prudential). These are the clearest, most precisely operationalised RegTech/SupTech definitions in the corpus — directly citable for any consulting deliverable or knowledge product addressing "regulation through IT" (a category the vault's current knowledge products do not yet explicitly separate from "regulation of AI").

### Institutional work framework — organisational field and institutional logics

Cites DiMaggio & Powell's (1983) organisational-field definition (verbatim-identical citation already used in [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F122_Scott_Institutional_Theory_Research_Program_2004|F122]] and [[04_Knowledge_Products/Wiki_Intake/F125_Alaimo_From_People_to_Objects_Digital_Transformation_Fields_2022|F125]] — a third corpus node this session independently anchoring to the same DiMaggio & Powell 1983 source) and Thornton & Ocasio's (1999) institutional logics definition, applied to a "framework of institutional work on regulating of and through IT." Explicitly names "lobbying... bribery and agency capture (Hawley, 2021)" as institutional-work mechanisms — a sharper, less euphemistic vocabulary than most of the corpus's existing coordination-mechanism language, worth flagging for any future PRIS discussion of *why* Regulatory_Parallelism persists (regulatory capture/lobbying as an alternative or complementary explanation to the corpus's existing "no coordination mechanism exists" framing).

### Conceptual ontology (Figure 1) and research-question taxonomy (Table 4)

A formal UML class diagram ontology for "regulation of and through IT," paired with a table of regulatory- and business-domain research questions for future IS studies — directly relevant as a citable methodological precedent for treating regulatory instruments as IS research objects (the same warrant the existing Shared Anchor already establishes for P2/P3, per that anchor's own framing: "regulatory frameworks are constructible as IS objects").
