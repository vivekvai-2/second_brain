## Classification

Senior legal analytical report from France's apex financial markets legal advisory body — the most detailed analysis of EU AI Act application to BFSI available in the corpus. Not a regulatory instrument itself, but a 77-page legal analysis equivalent to a practitioner law firm major report with institutional authority. Format B — valuable as EU AI Act application map for financial institutions rather than as a primary regulatory standard. Strong fsQCA relevance: provides detailed EU configurational data on how a rules-based, AI-Act jurisdiction structures AI governance obligations for BFSI actors.

## Three-Section Structure

**Section I — Existing regulatory framework for AI in sector-specific regulations:**
- AI as a tool for regulated financial services (credit institutions, payment/e-money, investment firms, insurance, asset management)
- New risks from AI: bias, opacity, third-party dependency
- AI governance obligations per sector
- Customer protection: explainability, transparency, liability
- Prudential regulation for AI; data protection frameworks; third-party supervision

**Section II — EU AI Act for Financial Institutions:**
- Scope (output-generation definition; extraterritorial application)
- Risk-based classification: prohibited/high/limited/minimal risk tiers
- Four roles for financial institutions: Provider, Deployer, Importer, Distributor
- Obligations and liability regimes per role
- Recognition of existing financial supervisory authorities as competent authorities under AI Act

**Section III — Coordinating EU AI Act with Financial Sector Regulations:**
- MiFID II, CRD, Solvency II, GDPR interactions with EU AI Act
- Lex specialis provisions and overlap management

## Reusable Findings

1. **EU AI Act four-role framework for BFSI (feeds P3/S3/S4):** HCJP's most immediately usable contribution is its mapping of financial institution roles under EU AI Act — Provider (builds AI), Deployer (uses AI), Importer (imports non-EU AI), Distributor. Most Indian BFSI firms using third-party AI (AWS Bedrock, Azure OpenAI, Google Vertex) are **Deployers** — with defined obligations including impact assessments, human oversight, transparency disclosure, and complaint mechanisms. This is the clearest available legal mapping of what "deployer" obligations mean in practice for a bank or insurer.

2. **Credit scoring and insurance underwriting as high-risk AI (feeds P3/fsQCA):** Under EU AI Act Article 6 + Annex III, AI used in credit scoring and insurance risk assessment is classified HIGH RISK — requiring: technical documentation, conformity assessment, registration in EU AI database, transparency to affected individuals, and post-market monitoring. This is the EU configurational data point for fsQCA: "presence of statutory high-risk classification for credit/insurance AI" — India does not yet have an equivalent statutory classification (RBI FREE-AI and SEBI guidance use voluntary principles, not statutory high-risk designation).

3. **Extraterritoriality: EU AI Act reach into Indian BFSI (feeds P4/Doctrinal):** HCJP confirms EU AI Act applies to AI systems placed on the market or put into service **in the EU** — regardless of where the AI developer or deployer is established. Indian AMCs marketing to EU investors, Indian banks with EU branches, and fintech platforms processing EU customer data may face EU AI Act compliance obligations. For P4 (doctrinal), this creates a direct interaction between EU AI Act and India's DPDP Act: an Indian fintech firm operating in both markets must simultaneously manage EU AI Act obligations (transparency, impact assessment) and India DPDP Rules (consent, data localisation).

4. **Explainability as customer right (feeds S1/Accountability/Transparency):** HCJP documents that the right to explanation of AI decisions in financial services is embedded in both EU AI Act and existing MiFID II/CRD frameworks — it is not a new AI Act innovation but a pre-existing financial consumer protection obligation now reinforced at AI-system level. This framing (explainability as consumer right, not just risk management practice) is analytically distinct from how RBI and SEBI have framed explainability (as a supervisory/governance tool). The consumer-rights framing is stronger and more enforceable — a gap between India and EU AI governance design.

5. **MiFID II/CRD/Solvency II as pre-existing AI governance layer (feeds S3/Institutional_Coherence):** HCJP's Section III analysis shows that EU financial sector regulations already required governance, transparency, and accountability for algorithmic/AI tools before the EU AI Act — the AI Act adds a layer on top of pre-existing sector-specific obligations. This "layered regulation" model contrasts with India's pattern: Indian sectoral AI governance (RBI FREE-AI, SEBI AI/ML reporting) exists alongside but mostly independently of broader principles frameworks (NITI, MeitY), with limited explicit cross-referencing or lex specialis mapping.

## fsQCA Configurational Data

|---|---|---|
| Statutory AI risk classification (credit/insurance) | Yes — EU AI Act Annex III | No — voluntary principles only |
| Legally binding AI obligations for financial deployers | Yes — EU AI Act + MiFID/CRD | No — RBI FREE-AI "should" language |
| Consumer right to AI explanation | Yes — AI Act + existing MiFID | No — no statutory consumer right yet |
| Extraterritorial application | Yes | Partial (DPDP Act has some extraterritoriality) |
